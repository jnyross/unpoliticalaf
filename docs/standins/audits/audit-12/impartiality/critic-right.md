# Critic (right-lean case): audit 12

Brief: make the strongest honest case that the built site leans right, meaning that its choices would nudge a newcomer towards right-leaning conclusions. I read only the built pages in `site/`: `index.html`, `spending.html`, `taxes.html`, `who-pays.html`, `household-tax.html`, `life/index.html`, `methods.html`, and the default comparison page they link to, `life/compare/2011-2019.html`. I also checked one published CSV that the spending page links to, `csv/spending-areas/…-billion-2025-26-prices.csv`.

I found ten examples worth listing. The first seven are strong. Examples 8 to 10 are real but weaker, and a fair reader could call them defensible.

---

## 1. "Who pays?" opens with the top 1%'s share of Income Tax, the most progressive measure there is

- **Where:** home page, card "Who pays?". The same figure is the lead hero figure on `who-pays.html`.
- **Exact words:** "**27.2%** of all Income Tax for the year was owed by the top 1% of Income Tax payers in 2023 to 2024. They had 12.4% of all Income Tax payers' income." The `who-pays.html` lede goes on: "Higher-income people pay most Income Tax." Section 1 then lists the shares, ending with "Bottom 50%: share of Income Tax 9.9%".
- **What a newcomer takes away:** the rich carry the tax system and half of taxpayers contribute almost nothing. That is the standard right-of-centre "top earners pay for everything" talking point.
- **Why it is slanted:** Income Tax is the most progressive tax, and it raised £329bn of the £1,105bn total (the site says so only lower down, under "Limits"). On the site's own all-taxes figures, the shares of income are almost flat: lowest fifth 36.7%, highest fifth 36.2%. To answer "who pays?", the site picks the single tax that makes the top look most burdened and puts it first. The all-taxes answer sits on another page.
- **Neutral choice:** lead "Who pays?" with all taxes as a share of income by fifth (36.7% / 30.7% / 30.2% / 31.7% / 36.2%). Put the Income Tax shares beside it, labelled as one tax. Alternatively, show the two side by side with equal weight on the home card.

## 2. Hero cards on "Who pays?" show the cash amounts paid by the richest and poorest fifths, not the shares

- **Where:** `who-pays.html`, the hero figures. Section 2 has a bar chart of the same figures.
- **Exact words:** "**£51,903** a year in all taxes, on average, by the households of the highest-income fifth" next to "**£8,625** a year in all taxes … lowest-income fifth". It is followed by "For tax as a share of household income, see Tax and income."
- **What a newcomer takes away:** the top fifth pays six times as much as the bottom fifth, so the system must lean heavily on the well-off.
- **Why it is slanted:** both fifths pay about the same share of income: 36.2% for the top and 36.7% for the bottom. A cash amount mainly reflects the size of the income. The site's own `household-tax.html` says the shares are "similar". The fact that undercuts the cash framing is moved to another page behind a link, and the cash framing gets the headline position.
- **Neutral choice:** show the cash and the share together in the hero, for example "£51,903 (36.2% of income) vs £8,625 (36.7% of income)". Or lead the page with the shares.

## 3. "More in benefits than they pay in taxes": the net-recipient framing

- **Where:** `who-pays.html`, section 4, "Who receives more in benefits than they pay in taxes?"
- **Exact words:** "In 2023/24, **53.3% of people** lived in households that received more in benefits and services than they paid in taxes." The chart starts at 37.0% in 1977. The headline is "The share for people in retired households was higher … in every year".
- **What a newcomer takes away:** most of the country takes out more than it puts in, and the share has been rising since 1977. This is close to the "makers and takers" or "half the country is a net drain" argument.
- **Why it is slanted:** the measure counts the NHS and state schools as "benefits" received. On that definition, almost any family with children or anyone who uses the NHS ends up as a "net recipient". The site's own caveats say Corporation Tax is not counted against any household, so the two sides "do not add up". It also says the figure is "one year at a time" and "does not follow people through their lives". Those caveats sit under "More on". The 53.3% headline does not need to be on a "Who pays?" page at all. Its presence and its section title invite a moral reading.
- **Neutral choice:** leave this series out of "Who pays?". If it stays, title it with what it measures, for example "Taxes paid compared with cash benefits and services used, one year at a time". Lead with the lifetime caveat. Show cash benefits separately from services in kind.

## 4. The default "compare two years" is 2011 against 2019, exactly the Coalition and Conservative years, and the gains are framed as recovery

- **Where:** `life/index.html`, "Compare two years": "Start with **2011 compared with 2019** … 2011 came soon after a recession and 2019 was the last year before the pandemic." The default page is `life/compare/2011-2019.html`.
- **Exact words on the default page:** employment "went up from 70.5% … to 76.1%"; pay "went up from £735 … to £749"; holidays abroad "+30%"; "Largest changes" lists employment of people aged 50 to 64 (+7.7 points), women's employment (+6.8 points) and holiday visits (+37%).
- **What a newcomer takes away:** the 2010s were a decade of rising employment, rising real pay and more holidays. It also reads as if the starting point was a recession inherited from before.
- **Why it is slanted:** every first-time visitor sees the same pair of years, chosen by the site, and it brackets one political era almost exactly. It starts at a low point for pay and employment just after the 2008–09 crash. The sentence "2011 came soon after a recession" frames the start as a trough, and the window ends before Covid. Pay in 2011 (£735) and 2019 (£749) was below the 2008 peak the site shows elsewhere (£771). The default view never shows that. The stated reason is "the earliest and latest years in which all five topics can be compared". That is a technical reason, but 2008–2019 or 2011–2025 were also available for most topics.
- **Neutral choice:** default to the earliest and latest years available (for example 2008 or 2009 against 2025), or to no preset pair. Leave out the sentence describing 2011 as just after a recession.

## 5. The ten-year spending window starts at the low point of the 2010–15 cuts, so every service looks as if it grew

- **Where:** `spending.html`, "Change by area over ten years: 2015-16 to 2025-26". It sits under the reassurance "The two years are a fixed ten years apart, not chosen for the result."
- **Exact words:** "Housing and community amenities 64% rise", "Health 34% rise", "Public order and safety (police, courts, prisons, fire) 33% rise", "Defence 29% rise", "Education 7% rise". Only "Recreation, culture and religion" falls (-4%).
- **What a newcomer takes away:** spending rose across the board after inflation, so complaints of "austerity" or "cuts" do not fit the numbers.
- **Why it is slanted:** 2015-16 is roughly the bottom of the post-2010 cuts. In the site's own published series (2025-26 prices, `csv/spending-areas`), comparing 2009-10 with 2015-16: housing and community amenities fell from £25.0bn to £13.7bn, public order and safety from £52.1bn to £41.9bn, and education from £135.2bn to £117.8bn. Housing (£22.5bn) and education (£125.7bn) are still below their 2009-10 level in 2025-26. A fixed window is not neutral when its start happens to fall at a trough. Calling it "not chosen for the result" protects the choice from scrutiny. The amounts are also not adjusted for population, even though the site quotes a population of 69.5 million. That makes the rises look larger per person than they are.
- **Neutral choice:** show the full 2003-04 to 2025-26 series for each area as the main view, or include a second window from 2009-10. Give per-person amounts after inflation alongside the totals.

## 6. The ONS reassurance that VAT is not really regressive, taken from a 2017 bulletin the ONS no longer repeats

- **Where:** `household-tax.html`, explanation box "Why can indirect taxes be a large share of income for lower-income households?"
- **Exact words (the quote):** "Some households, particularly those at the lower end of the income distribution, may have annual expenditure that exceeds their annual income … During periods of low income, these households may maintain their standard of living by funding their expenditure from savings or borrowing, thereby adjusting their lifetime consumption." The site adds: "This is from the ONS bulletin for April 2015 to March 2016 … the latest ONS bulletin does not repeat this explanation."
- **What a newcomer takes away:** the lowest fifth's high VAT burden (21.0% of income) is mostly a statistical artefact of people living off savings, so taxes on spending are not really hard on the poor.
- **Why it is slanted:** the site's principle is to quote official explanations, yet here it reaches past the current bulletin to a 2017 one. The one explanation it chooses for the most left-relevant finding on the page (indirect tax weighs most on the poor) is the one that softens it. Nothing in the box gives the plainer reading that lower-income households spend a larger share of their income. The box below it ("Why the same tax can look bigger or smaller", showing 21.0% / 24.9% / 16.5%) points the same way, towards the smallest denominator.
- **Neutral choice:** use the current bulletin's explanation, or none. If the lifetime-consumption point stays, give it equal space alongside the plain spending-share point.

## 7. National debt, borrowing and tax "per household"

- **Where:** `taxes.html`, "Borrowing is not the same as debt" and "For scale". There are parallel per-household figures on `spending.html`.
- **Exact words:** "Debt … £2,917bn … about **£41,978 per person (£100,569 per household)**". "Borrowing was £1,933 per person, or **£4,630 per household**." "Tax raised … about **£38,089 per household**, or £732 a week." Spending: "**£46,895 per household**, or £902 a week."
- **What a newcomer takes away:** every household owes about £100,000 and pays about £38,000 a year in tax, so the state is enormous and the debt is a personal burden.
- **Why it is slanted:** "your share of the national debt" is a long-standing rhetorical device of fiscal hawks. The caveat is there ("not anyone's bill: it is owed by the public sector"), but the £100,569 number is the memorable part. Per-household tax divides Corporation Tax and other business taxes across households. The site's own survey measure puts the real household average at £23,106. Large round figures per household make the state look heavy, and the caveat cannot undo that effect.
- **Neutral choice:** give debt as a share of GDP only (93.3%), with its history. Drop the per-household debt and borrowing figures. For tax per household, use the ONS household figure (£23,106), not a division of every tax.

## 8. "Spending was higher than receipts in 68 of the 78 years": a headline about overspending

- **Where:** `taxes.html`, chart "Receipts and spending since 1948-49". The after-inflation version reads "Spending was higher than receipts in 65 of the 71 years".
- **What a newcomer takes away:** governments habitually spend beyond their means, which supports the case for spending restraint.
- **Why it is slanted:** the chart could carry any title. The site picks the count of deficit years, a framing that suits the right, rather than describing the paths of the two lines. Receipts are forecast to reach 42.9% of GDP, about the highest since 1949-50, which is just as headline-worthy, and the chart is silent on it. Elsewhere the site's headlines describe the shape of a line, for example "rose until 2009-10, fell until 2018-19…". Here the headline is a verdict-shaped count.
- **Neutral choice:** a descriptive title such as "Receipts and spending as a share of GDP, 1948-49 to 2025-26, with OBR forecasts to 2031-32".

## 9. "Everyday life" is measured with upbeat, affluence-type indicators, each with a flattering rank

- **Where:** home page "Everyday life, year by year", and `life/index.html`.
- **Exact words:** the topics are Work, Earnings, Housing (ownership), Holidays (abroad) and Class sizes. "75.3%, higher than in 51 of the other 54 years shown." "Visits abroad … 2023: 126, higher than in 40 of the other 43 years shown." Housing is led on the home page by "65% of households in England owned their home". Earnings are compared "from 2021 … about the same", which avoids the longer stagnation (the scale shows a high of £771 in 2008).
- **What a newcomer takes away:** life in Britain is broadly good and improving, with high employment, more foreign holidays and stable ownership. That suits a right-of-centre, status-quo reading.
- **Why it is slanted:** five indicators stand for "everyday life", and one of them is foreign holidays, while poverty, food-bank use, NHS waiting times, homelessness and the house-price-to-earnings ratio are absent. The ratio exists on the site: the compare page shows 6.74 to 7.73. The "higher than in N of the other years" phrasing presents each latest figure as a near-record. The home housing card leads with ownership rather than private renting (14% to 19%) or affordability.
- **Neutral choice:** include at least one measure of hardship or of access to public services. Lead housing with tenure split and affordability together. Drop the "higher than in N of the other years" ranking, or apply it to every series, including ones that show deterioration.
- **Caveat on my own case:** the earnings rule (compare only within one survey coding) has a real methodological basis, so part of this example is weaker.

## 10. Gross debt interest is put up front, with a dedicated explanation box

- **Where:** home page "Where does public money go?" and `spending.html`.
- **Exact words:** "General public services (£13) includes debt interest (£11 of the £13)"; "interest on government debt, £11 of every £100 spent on services". A full ONS quote box: "Why did interest on government debt rise in 2022-23? … £106.6 billion, £34.0 billion (46.9%) more".
- **What a newcomer takes away:** debt interest is one of the biggest items in the budget, more than defence (£5) or police and courts (£5). That reinforces worries about debt.
- **Why it is slanted:** the site chooses the gross figure. It admits that the "net" figure the OBR and IFS quote is lower, but never gives that number. Debt interest is the only line item inside a spending area that gets its own home-page mention and its own explanation box. No such treatment goes to, say, the State Pension within social protection, which gets one sentence ("£155bn").
- **Neutral choice:** show gross and net debt interest side by side as numbers, or use the OBR's net figure, since the site uses the OBR elsewhere. Give pensions and debt interest the same treatment.

### Considered but not listed

- **"Tax burden"** (`taxes.html`: "a share news reports often call the 'tax burden'"). It introduces a loaded, right-coded term. It is attributed to news reports and explained, so it is weak.
- **HMRC and ONS quotes on fiscal drag** ("Why are more people paying Income Tax?", "Why did direct taxes rise in 2023/24?"). They stress that taxes are creeping up. They read as criticism of the government of the day rather than as a right/left lean, so they are inconclusive.
- **The HMRC quote on average Income Tax rates** (4.8% up to 29.2% by income). It adds to the picture of a steeply progressive system (see example 1), but taken alone it is factual and relevant.
- **The ONS redistribution quote** ("12.2 times … reduced to 3.3 times"). It could be read as "the system already redistributes a lot", but it could just as well support the left. Neutral.

### Overall

The clearest pattern is on the "who pays" pages (examples 1–3). The site keeps leading with Income Tax-only and cash-amount measures that make the well-off look heavily burdened. The measure that shows the system as roughly flat (all taxes as a share of income) gets second billing, on a different page. The second pattern is the choice of time windows (examples 4, 5 and 9): the 2011→2019 default comparison and the 2015-16 spending baseline both start at troughs, so the 2010s look like a period of improvement and the 2010–15 cuts drop out of view. Each choice is individually defensible and caveated. Together they would nudge a newcomer to the right of where the full data sits.
