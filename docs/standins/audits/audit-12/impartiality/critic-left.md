# Critic: the case that the site leans left

Pages read: `index.html`, `spending.html`, `taxes.html`, `who-pays.html`,
`household-tax.html`, `life/index.html`, `methods.html`, plus pages they link
to: `international.html`, `borrowing.html`, `who-pays-households.html`,
`glossary.html`, `life/2019.html`, `life/2025.html`.

Overall: the site is careful, and most figures are given with balancing
context. The left-leaning case is real but modest. It rests mostly on which
caveats get attached to which figures, and which comparison group or
denominator is chosen. Few of these are clear errors. I found ten examples
worth listing. Numbers 8 to 10 are weak, and I say so for each.

---

## 1. Homepage: "taxes take more from the poorest fifth" with no warning

**Page:** `index.html`, card "How much household income goes on tax?" (repeated
as the lead on `household-tax.html`).

**Exact words:** "33.6% of gross household income … By income fifth: Lowest-income
fifth 36.7% … Highest-income fifth 36.2%. For the lowest-income fifth, most of
this is indirect tax such as VAT (21.0% of income)". The household-tax page
leads with "The share was similar for the lowest-income fifth of people (36.7%)
and the highest-income fifth (36.2%)".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** the UK tax system is flat or regressive, and
the poorest are taxed as hard as the richest, or harder. This is a common
argument for higher taxes on the rich.

**Why it is slanted:** the poorest fifth's figure is pushed up by two things the
site knows about but keeps off the homepage:
- it uses *gross* income, which includes benefits;
- it divides spending taxes by income, and the ONS says low-income households
  often spend more than their income, from savings or borrowing.

The site's own page shows that indirect tax for this group is 16.5% of their
*spending*, against 21.0% of income. The ONS's spending caveat appears only
on the inner page, in a quote dated 2017. The homepage's five bars are the only
"who pays" figures with a chart there, and they are the ones that best support a
left argument.

**Neutral choice:** on the homepage, say in one line that spending taxes look
large for the lowest fifth partly because some of them spend more than their
income, or show the £ amounts (£8,625 against £51,903), which the who-pays page
leads with.

## 2. The "tax burden" is put in scare quotes and explained away

**Pages:** `taxes.html` ("Receipts and spending since 1948-49") and the
`glossary.html` entry “Tax burden”.

**Exact words:** "Taxes alone were 36.0% of GDP in 2025-26, a share news reports
often call the 'tax burden'. It compares all tax … with the size of the economy,
so it is not anyone's tax bill". Glossary: "This site does not use the name
itself … when this share goes up, it does not mean everyone pays more".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** worry about a high or rising tax take is a
media idea, not something real.

**Why it is slanted:** "tax burden" is the OBR's own standard phrase, not a
tabloid one. The site distances itself from the term most used to argue
*against* higher taxes. It adds a "does not mean everyone pays more" disclaimer,
but gives no matching "does not mean everyone gets more" when *spending* as a
share of GDP is shown. The taxes page also never says plainly where today's level
stands historically. Its own chart text has receipts rising to 40.1% of GDP, with
a forecast of 42.9% for 2031-32, against a series high of 43.3% in 1949-50. That
makes the forecast close to a post-war record, but no sentence says so. The site
does not hesitate to give similar rankings elsewhere, for example "higher than in
51 of the other 54 years" for employment.

**Neutral choice:** use the OBR term without quotation marks. Add a plain ranking
sentence, such as "the highest share since …" or "higher than in N of the 78
years". Apply the same kind of caveat to spending-to-GDP, or drop it from both.

## 3. National debt: "owed by the public sector, not by people or households"

**Page:** `taxes.html`, "Borrowing is not the same as debt".

**Exact words:** "Debt … £2,917bn … about £41,978 per person (£100,569 per
household). That is the debt divided by everyone, an average, not anyone's bill:
it is owed by the public sector, not by people or households."

**What a newcomer takes from it:** the national debt is not really their
problem.

**Why it is slanted:** the public sector's debts are serviced and repaid from
taxes, which people and households pay. Saying it is "not owed by people"
answers the main fiscal-conservative argument ("every household owes £100k")
without saying so. The site applies "not anyone's bill" wording to tax and debt
per person. The matching "per person" spending figure (£19,574) only gets the
milder "not the amount spent on any one person".

**Neutral choice:** "an average across everyone; the debt is owed by the public
sector and is serviced from public revenue, mainly taxes." Or drop the comment
and leave the per-person figure as a plain average, as is done for spending.

## 4. G7 is chosen as the peer group, which makes the UK look low-tax and low-debt

**Pages:** `international.html` and `borrowing.html`; both are linked from the
homepage.

**Exact words:** international: "Among them, the UK had the second lowest tax as a
share of GDP in 2024 (of the 6 with a figure for that year)". Borrowing, first
sentence: "UK government debt was 101.3% of GDP, the second lowest of the seven
G7 countries".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** the UK is a lightly taxed, lightly indebted
country, so there is room to tax and borrow more.

**Why it is slanted:** the G7 includes high-tax France and Italy and very
high-debt Japan and Italy. Against the wider OECD set, which the site also has,
the picture changes. UK debt (92.5% on the OECD measure) is well above the OECD
average of 77.1%, but that comparison sits inside a second tab. On tax, the UK
is above the OECD average (34.4% against 34.1%). The site leads with the G7
"second lowest" rank on both pages, and the debt page leads with it in its first
sentence.

**Neutral choice:** lead with the OECD average on both pages, or give both peer
groups in the first sentence ("second lowest in the G7, above the OECD average").

## 5. Corporation Tax is treated as falling on no household; VAT is "passed on"

**Pages:** `glossary.html`, `who-pays.html` section 2, `household-tax.html`
limits.

**Exact words:** glossary, VAT: "The shop or business collects it, but the cost
is mostly passed on in prices." Glossary, Corporation Tax: "It is paid by
companies, so the household figures on this site do not include it."

**What a newcomer takes from it:** VAT hits ordinary families, while Corporation
Tax costs households nothing. Taxing companies looks free and taxing spending
looks regressive.

**Why it is slanted:** the site treats tax incidence one way for VAT and the
opposite way for Corporation Tax. Official and academic work, including the OBR,
HMT and IFS, treats part of Corporation Tax as falling on workers through lower
wages and on shareholders, including pension funds. "It is paid by companies, so…"
is presented as a fact, when it is really a choice the ONS makes about what to
count.

**Neutral choice:** "Companies pay it, but its cost falls in part on
shareholders, workers or customers. The ONS does not allocate it to households,
so it is left out here."

## 6. Who pays: the right-leaning headline is immediately qualified, the left-leaning one is not

**Pages:** `index.html` "Who pays?" card; `who-pays.html` lead.

**Exact words (home):** "27.2% of all Income Tax for the year was owed by the top
1% … They had 12.4% of all Income Tax payers' income. Every household also pays
taxes on spending, such as VAT."

**What a newcomer takes from it:** the top-1% figure is only part of the story,
and everyone pays.

**Why it is slanted:** the one figure that supports the view that "the rich
already pay a lot" comes with a counterweight in the same card. The household-tax
card under it, which supports the opposite view (example 1), has no counterweight
about benefits in gross income or about spending above income. The site
qualifies one side and not the other.

**Neutral choice:** both cards with a balancing line, or neither.

## 7. "Net recipients" figures are steered towards pensioners and hedged

**Page:** `who-pays.html`, section 4, and `who-pays-households.html`.

**Exact words:** "53.3% of people lived in households that received more in
benefits and services than they paid in taxes. That was 90.1% of people in
retired households and 45.8% in non-retired households." Then: "Households with
children count the value of their state schooling as a benefit" and "the same
person can be on either side at different times".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** the "more people take out than put in" figure
is mostly about pensioners and schoolchildren, not working-age welfare.

**Why it is slanted:** the site breaks the figure down only by retired against
non-retired, which moves attention to pensioners. Section 4 states the 1977
figure for everyone (37.0%) but never sets it next to 53.3%, so the rise is left
for the reader to find in a table. The site also uses "Social protection
(pensions, benefits, social care)" and puts pensions first, so working-age
benefits are never seen as a separate line on the spending page. Pensions get
their own figure (£155bn); working-age welfare does not.

**Neutral choice:** "53.3%, up from 37.0% in 1977, the first year of the series."
On the spending page, add the working-age benefits line next to the pensions one.

## 8. Year-page housing chart: private renting from its lowest point (weak)

**Pages:** `life/2019.html`, `life/2025.html` (each year page); `life/index.html`.

**Exact words:** the year-page chart is "Households renting privately, 2008-09 to
2025-26 … The scale runs from 14.2% (2008-09) to 20.3% (2016-17) and does not
start at zero". The life index adds: "In 2008-09, 14% rented privately."

**What a newcomer takes from it:** a sharp rise in private renting ("generation
rent").

**Why it is slanted, and why it is weak:** the series starts at its lowest point,
and the axis is not zeroed, which makes the rise look large. The index page uses
the longer home-ownership series from 1981 for its chart, so the site is
inconsistent rather than hiding anything. It is weak because 2008-09 is where the
series the site uses begins.

**Neutral choice:** on year pages, use the same ownership-from-1981 series as the
index, or show renting against a zero baseline.

## 9. "Everyday life" leans on hardship and inequality measures (weak)

**Pages:** `life/2025.html` and other year pages.

**Exact words:** among the figures listed are "Median house price ÷ median
full-time earnings … 7.55 times", "Full-time pay: top-tenth line ÷ bottom-tenth
line 3.0 times", "Families with dependent children renting privately" and
"Overcrowded households".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** everyday life is mainly about affordability,
inequality and crowding.

**Why it is slanted, and why it is weak:** these are standard measures, but the
list has no measures that tend to read well, such as household disposable income
or wealth, life satisfaction, or the share of homes with central heating or other
amenities. It is weak because the topics (work, pay, housing, holidays, class
sizes) are neutral, and holidays leans the other way.

**Neutral choice:** add one or two measures of living standards or wealth beside
the affordability and inequality ratios.

## 10. ONS quote on redistribution shows "the gap" only (weak)

**Page:** `household-tax.html`, "What do taxes and benefits do to the gap between
richer and poorer households?"

**Exact words:** "The richest fifth … (£116,600) was 12.2 times larger than the
poorest fifth (£9,600); however, this gap reduced to 3.3 times".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** "the gap" between rich and poor is the frame
for judging the tax system.

**Why it is weak:** the quote shows that taxes and benefits redistribute a lot,
which can support either side. I list it only because the question puts the
framing in inequality terms ("the gap between richer and poorer"), where a
neutral question would be "How much do taxes and benefits change household
incomes?"

**Neutral choice:** a question that does not assume what the reader should care
about.

## What did not hold up

These were checked and do not support a left lean:
- Debt interest is shown *gross* (the larger figure), with a note that the
  OBR/IFS net figure is lower. This goes against a left lean, if anything.
- The ten-year spending window is fixed and stated.
- Pay since 2021 is described as "about the same".
- HMRC and ONS quotes on frozen thresholds and fiscal drag are prominent. They
  criticise tax rises, which a left-leaning site would play down.
- The borrowing page says plainly that UK 10-year yields were "the highest of the
  G7".

I could find ten examples, but only 1 to 5 are strong. Numbers 6 and 7 are
moderate, and 8 to 10 are weak. Read as a whole, the site is closer to neutral
than to left-leaning. The clearest problem is uneven caveats: figures that could
alarm about tax or debt are qualified more than figures that could alarm about
inequality.
