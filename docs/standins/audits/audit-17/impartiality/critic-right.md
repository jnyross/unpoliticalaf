# Impartiality critic: the case that the site leans right

Audit 17. Site built from commit f113d8a. Pages read: index, spending, taxes,
who-pays, household-tax, life/index, methods, and the newer immigration,
defence, high-earners, leaving-uk, manifesto, productivity, welfare and
economy-options pages. I read only the built HTML.

This is the strongest honest case I could make that a newcomer would be nudged
towards right-of-centre conclusions. The site hedges a great deal: almost every
example below has a caveat somewhere on the page. The complaint is about what
is put first, what is chosen, and what is left out, not about false figures.
Seven of the ten examples come from the newer pages.

---

## 1. Who pays: "more in benefits than they pay in taxes" (who-pays.html, section 4)

**Exact words:** "In 2023/24, 53.3% of people lived in households that received
more in benefits and services than they paid in taxes. That was 90.1% of people
in retired households and 45.8% in non-retired households." The chart's title
is "People in households receiving more in benefits than they pay in taxes, 1977
to 2023/24".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** most people are "net recipients" who take
more than they put in, and the share is rising (37.0% in 1977). This is the
"makers and takers" framing that right-of-centre arguments about the size of the
state and welfare dependency rely on.

**Why it leans:** the "benefits" side counts the NHS and state schools as
things received. That makes almost every family with children, and almost every
pensioner, look like a net drain in any one year. The lifetime caveat ("one year
at a time … the same person can be on either side at different times") comes
after the headline, in the Limits. Corporation Tax and other taxes not
allocated to households are missing from the "taxes" side, which pushes the
share up further.

**Neutral choice:** headline the ONS's own redistribution result, which the
site quotes on household-tax.html but not here: the gap between the richest and
poorest fifth falls from 12.2 times to 3.3 times after taxes and benefits. Or
put the lifetime caveat into the headline sentence, and drop the "receiving
more … than they pay" wording from the chart title.

## 2. Leading with the top 1%'s Income Tax share (index.html "Who pays?"; who-pays.html headline)

**Exact words:** home page tile "27.2%. The top 1% of Income Tax payers owed
27.2% of all Income Tax in 2023 to 2024. They had 12.4% of all Income Tax
payers' income." who-pays.html opens with the same figure and then says:
"Higher-income people pay most Income Tax."

**What a newcomer takes from it:** the rich already carry the system, so asking
them to pay more is unfair. This is the standard right-of-centre answer to
"who pays?".

**Why it leans:** Income Tax is about £277bn of £976bn of all taxes in 2023-24
(the site's own Limits note). Across all taxes, ONS shows the top and bottom
fifths both paying about 36% of gross income: a broadly flat burden. The
Income-Tax-only figure is the most progressive-looking statistic available, and
it gets the first and biggest slot. The household-tax figures come second on
the home page and third on who-pays.

**Neutral choice:** lead "Who pays?" with all taxes as a share of income by
fifth, the ONS measure that covers every household, or show the two side by
side with equal weight. Drop "Higher-income people pay most Income Tax" as the
lead sentence, or pair it straight away with "and all groups pay a similar share
of income in all taxes".

## 3. High-earner flight: one-directional OBR evidence (high-earners.html, section 4)

**Exact words:** the page's only evidence on behaviour is three OBR quotes about
the 50p rate: "£4.9 billion of this would never materialise as they took steps
to reduce their taxable income … (e.g. … leaving the country)"; "the behavioural
response … had been greater than originally assumed"; "The estimated underlying
yield … was also reduced from £2.6 billion to £0.6 billion." The Limits add:
"The OBR's 50p review shows those responses can be large."

**What a newcomer takes from it:** taxing high earners more makes them leave or
hide their income, so it raises little. This is a core right-of-centre argument
against higher top rates.

**Why it leans:** the page is framed around leaving ("What if high earners left
the UK?", "What would leavers take with them?"), yet it gives no official
measure of how many high earners actually left after any tax change. All the
behaviour evidence points one way. The OBR's own point that even the revised
yield was still positive is not drawn out. The worked table of "1,000 / 10,000 /
100,000 leavers" makes the scenario feel concrete without any evidence that it
happens.

**Neutral choice:** title it "How much Income Tax do the highest earners pay?".
Present the leavers arithmetic as one scenario among others. Add any official
evidence on actual migration of top taxpayers, such as HMRC's non-dom outflow
figures, which the site already holds on leaving-uk.html and which show fewer
leaving and more tax paid. Or say plainly that no official figure measures it.

## 4. Immigration: the one fiscal estimate is a negative one (immigration.html)

**Exact words:** "What do migrants pay and cost in public money?" is answered
only by the MAC's Partner route analysis: "will incur a net lifetime fiscal
deficit of -£5.6bn". The next box quotes the OBR: "The fiscal impacts of
migration are likely to become less beneficial over time … become eligible for
welfare benefits." The public-services section quotes "reduce DEL spending per
person by £40 … and increase the pressure on those services".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** migrants cost money over their lifetimes,
draw benefits once settled, and strain services. Each is a right-of-centre
talking point.

**Why it leans:** the Family/Partner route is one of the least fiscally positive
routes. The page says it "does not describe every migrant" but offers no
estimate for any route that is positive over a lifetime, such as Skilled Worker
or high-wage migrants, which official bodies have modelled. The positive OBR
finding (receipts £18.0bn higher, borrowing £13.1bn lower) comes last, after
three boxes that read as costs. "Is immigration a good or a bad thing?" opens
the page and then puts the negative evidence first.

**Neutral choice:** show the range of official per-route or per-wage-level
lifetime estimates side by side (positive and negative), or leave the lifetime
section out until a representative one is available. Order the boxes so the
aggregate OBR fiscal result is not last.

## 5. Economy options: a supply-side, business-lobby menu (economy-options.html)

**Exact words:** the top-ranked option is "Planning reform, full expensing and
access to finance". Third is "Getting people on long-term sick leave back into
work". The budget list leads with "Keeping the state pension triple lock
(reforming it to earnings uprating): Saves about £16 billion a year" and
includes "Abolishing stamp duty on main homes". One source is the CBI.

**What a newcomer takes from it:** growth comes from tax breaks for business
investment, deregulated planning, getting the sick off benefits and into work,
and cutting stamp duty. That is close to a right-of-centre growth programme.

**Why it leans:** full expensing and stamp-duty abolition are associated with
Conservative and Reform policy. There is no option for public investment,
taxing wealth or capital gains more, a higher minimum wage, or a closer EU
relationship beyond "regulatory alignment". The EU option is given the smallest
single-source estimate (+0.6%), and the OBR's own estimate of the long-run cost
of leaving the EU's trading arrangements is not used. The methods page promises
"Official sources only", but this page leans on a business lobby (CBI) and an
industry plan.

**Neutral choice:** build the list from one consistent official source, such as
the OECD table alone, or include a balanced set of options from across the
spectrum, each with a comparable estimate. Label the triple lock option neutrally
("Changing triple lock uprating to earnings") rather than leading with the
saving.

## 6. Manifesto: only Labour is audited, and the selection favours breaches (manifesto.html)

**Exact words:** the page title is "Did Labour keep its 2024 manifesto?" The
first row of the ratings is "Will not increase taxes on working people: Not kept
on the plain wording". Four of the ten pledges are about tax. The home page also
asks "Did Labour keep its 2024 manifesto?".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** Labour broke its tax promises. This is the
opposition's main line of attack.

**Why it leans:** there is no matching page for the previous government's 2019
manifesto, so only one party's record is tested. The ten pledges are weighted
towards tax, where the disputes are, rather than towards pledges such as
workers' rights, the minimum wage or GB Energy. The ordering puts the worst
rating first.

**Neutral choice:** add the 2019 Conservative manifesto on the same method, or
frame the page as "Government pledges since 2024" with pledges picked by a fixed
rule. Order the pledges by manifesto page or by topic, not by tax first.

## 7. Defence: "Why can't we afford…?" and welfare as the competitor (index.html; defence.html)

**Exact words:** the home-page link reads "Why can't we afford to spend more on
defence?" On the defence page, "What does defence compete with?" lists "Social
protection (pensions, benefits) £33.2" first, with a bar chart in which defence
is darkened against the big social protection bar.

**What a newcomer takes from it:** more defence spending is desirable and is
being held back. The obvious place to find the money is the largest bar,
pensions and benefits. This is a hawkish, smaller-welfare-state framing.

**Why it leans:** the question on the home page assumes the answer. The page's
own title is more neutral ("What limits UK defence spending, and what would more
cost?"), but the newcomer sees the home-page wording. "Compete with" puts
defence against social security in particular, not against tax rises or
borrowing.

**Neutral choice:** use the page title on the home page, or "How much does the
UK spend on defence, and what would more cost?". Present the spending split
without the "compete with" heading.

## 8. Welfare: a tenfold rise headline from a 1955 base (welfare.html)

**Exact words:** the opening sentence: "Welfare spending, measured as social
security (benefits and the State Pension), was £31.7bn in 1955-56 and £307bn in
2021-22, both after inflation."

**What a newcomer takes from it:** welfare has ballooned nearly tenfold. That
feeds the right-of-centre "out-of-control welfare bill" narrative.

**Why it leans:** the real-terms total over 66 years mostly reflects a bigger
economy, a bigger population and many more pensioners. The fairer measures, the
share of GDP (5.0% to 10.8%, falling since 2012-13) and pensioners' share
falling from 59% to 54%, come second and third. Calling the State Pension
"welfare" in the title inflates the figure that newcomers link with working-age
benefits.

**Neutral choice:** lead with the share of GDP, the measure that allows for
growth, and call the page "Social security spending" rather than "Welfare".

## 9. Debt per household and the "spending exceeded receipts" headline (taxes.html; spending.html)

**Exact words:** taxes.html has a headline tile "£134bn borrowed to cover
spending above receipts", and "about £41,978 per person (£100,569 per
household)" of debt. The chart heading reads "Spending was higher than receipts
in 68 of the 78 years from 1948-49 to 2025-26". spending.html says "total spending
in 2025-26 was 54% higher than in 2003-04" and rose from "38.7% to 44.3%" of
GDP.

**What a newcomer takes from it:** the state spends beyond its means and has
grown, and every household is £100,000 in hock. These are fiscal-hawk framings.

**Why it leans:** debt per household is a vivid figure that the site itself
says is "not anyone's bill", yet it is printed beside one. The "68 of 78 years"
sentence is the chart's lead finding, chosen over, for example, debt falling as
a share of GDP for decades after 1948. The 2003-04 start for spending growth
leaves out the lower levels of earlier decades and the 1980s peak.

**Neutral choice:** give debt as a share of GDP only. Describe the receipts and
spending chart by its shape, not by a count of deficit years. Use the longer
OBR series for spending as a share of GDP so the 2025-26 level sits in a
70-year context.

## 10. Emigration "Yes", and the run of new topics (leaving-uk.html; index.html)

**Exact words:** "Are people leaving the UK? Yes, a large number of people
leave the UK each year, and the official estimate has risen since 2022." The
page then gives the "136,000 more Brits left than arrived" figure. On the home
page, the new questions sit together: defence affordability, high earners
leaving, people leaving the UK, whether Labour kept its manifesto, and whether
immigration is good or bad.

**What a newcomer takes from it:** Britain is losing people, and the list of
questions the site chooses to answer mirrors the current right-of-centre media
agenda: tax flight, emigration, broken Labour promises, immigration costs and
welfare growth.

**Why it leans:** the page itself says ONS warns "it is not correct to say that
our migration statistics show a recent rise in emigration of British
nationals", and that total emigration fell by 38,000 in the latest year. Yet
the lead word is "Yes" and "has risen since 2022". There are no new pages on the
questions the left tends to ask, such as poverty, inequality, public-service
waiting or the minimum wage. The methods page admits that "Poverty and hardship
have official series that the site does not show yet".

**Neutral choice:** open with the number and the latest change ("About 642,000
people left in 2025, 38,000 fewer than the year before") with no "Yes". Balance
the topic pages with official series on poverty, inequality and public-service
outcomes, or explain on the home page how the topics were chosen.

---

**Count:** ten examples. Examples 1 to 5 are the strongest. Examples 8 to 10 are
weaker, because the pages also give the neutral measure, just lower down.
Productivity.html showed no clear lean in either direction, so none of the
examples come from it.
