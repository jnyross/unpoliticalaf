# Impartiality critic: the case that the site leans left

Audit 17. Site built from commit f113d8a. I read the built pages in `site/` only: the headline pages (`index.html`, `spending.html`, `taxes.html`, `who-pays.html`, `household-tax.html`, `life/index.html`, `methods.html`) and the new pages (`immigration.html`, `defence.html`, `high-earners.html`, `leaving-uk.html`, `manifesto.html`, `productivity.html`, `welfare.html`, `economy-options.html`).

My job was to make the strongest honest case that the site leans left. Most pages are careful and many choices also cut the other way: the 50p behavioural review, the MAC Partner-route deficit, the "53.3% receive more than they pay" figure, and the richest fifth paying 46% of household taxes. Those are left out below because they don't support a left lean. Seven of the ten examples come from the new pages.

---

## 1. `manifesto.html`: the National Insurance pledge is rated more kindly than the site's own named sources rate it

- **Words:** In the summary table: "Will not increase National Insurance : Partly kept; readings differ". The section body then says: "Full Fact rates the pledge 'Not kept'" and "The Institute for Fiscal Studies says the freeze and the cap breach the pledge."
- **What a newcomer takes from it:** The rise in employer NI, from 13.8% to 15% with the threshold cut to £5,000, the largest tax-raising measure of the parliament, only half-breached a pledge. The two independent judges the page names both say "Not kept", but the site lands softer than either of them. The headline answer, "Partly, and most of it cannot be judged yet. … Three are kept", sets a generous frame before the reader opens a single pledge. The same softening shows elsewhere. Housing is "Not yet testable" even though the government's own estimate is 29% of the target after more than two years and the housing secretary called the chance of meeting it "slim". Defence is "Kept so far" despite a change of definition that Full Fact compares with one Labour itself once criticised.
- **Neutral choice:** Where both named independent arbiters say "Not kept", the table rating should be "Not kept (the government disputes this)", or the two readings should appear in the table itself, not only further down. For housing, "Off track so far" would match Full Fact and the minister's own words.

## 2. `leaving-uk.html`: figures from before the non-dom change are used to reassure readers about it

- **Words:** In "The short answer": "HMRC figures for non-domiciled taxpayers show fewer people leaving that group in the 2024 to 2025 tax year than the year before. Income Tax paid by that group rose." Further down, the page itself says these figures end "before a new residence-based regime began on 6 April 2025".
- **What a newcomer takes from it:** Fears of a non-dom exodus are not borne out, and the tax take is up. But the data covers the year before the abolition took effect, so it cannot speak to the claim people are actually making. Putting it in the five-line short answer without that caveat turns an irrelevant reassurance into the headline. The page also gives prominent space to an ONS rebuttal, "it is not correct to say that our migration statistics show a recent rise in emigration of British nationals", which answers a claim mostly made from the right.
- **Neutral choice:** In the short answer, either leave the HMRC line out or attach its timing caveat in the same sentence ("…in 2024 to 2025, the last year before the regime changed, so they cannot show its effect").

## 3. `economy-options.html`: the menu and its sources lean towards centre-left options

- **Words:** The ten options include "Ambitious alignment of rules with the EU", "Net migration higher than the OBR's baseline" (ranked second), "More childcare that brings mothers back into work", "Reviewing tax reliefs" ("the reliefs cost £207 billion"), "Keeping the state pension triple lock (reforming it to earnings uprating)" and grid investment. Three sources are the Resolution Foundation.
- **What a newcomer takes from it:** The serious ways to grow the economy are closer ties with the EU, more migration, childcare and recovering tax reliefs. Several options often argued from the right are missing entirely: lower corporation or income tax rates, a smaller state or spending restraint, labour-market deregulation and domestic energy production. Only planning and full expensing represent that side. Getting more migration onto the list at all, ranked second on total GDP, is itself a framing choice, even though the page then admits it would rank last per person.
- **Neutral choice:** Choose the options from one neutral list (for example, all rows of OECD Table 1.1, or the OBR's and IFS's costed options) rather than mixing in think-tank scenarios. Where think tanks are used, balance them across the spectrum. Rank on GDP per person, the measure the page itself says matters for living standards, or show both rankings side by side.

## 4. `index.html` and `household-tax.html`: "the poorest pay the most tax" is a headline card

- **Words:** On the home page: "The lowest-income fifth paid 36.7% of its gross income in tax, and the highest-income fifth paid 36.2%". The bar chart's tallest bar is the lowest fifth, at 36.7%.
- **What a newcomer takes from it:** The tax system takes as much from the poorest as from the richest, or slightly more, which is a familiar argument for progressive tax reform. The figure depends on dividing by gross income for a group whose spending exceeds its income: on spending, the lowest fifth pays 16.5%, about the same as everyone else. ONS itself warns that lump sums of deferred tax inflate this fifth's direct taxes. The caveat is there, but it sits below a chart that is built to give the opposite impression.
- **Neutral choice:** Lead with the ONS's own headline for this release, which is about redistribution ("12.2 times … reduced to 3.3 times"), or show the shares on more than one denominator from the start, so the poorest fifth's figure isn't the visual peak.

## 5. `high-earners.html`: the loss from leavers is understated and then made to look small

- **Words:** "1,000 leavers from this group would take £1.1bn of tax with them, 0.39% of all Income Tax", followed by a yardstick: "2024-25 receipts were £35 billion (3.2%) higher than its March 2023 forecast … A gap of that size shows how far receipts can move".
- **What a newcomer takes from it:** Even a substantial departure of millionaires is a rounding error. But the page counts Income Tax only. Wealthy leavers also take Capital Gains Tax, Inheritance Tax, NI, VAT on their spending, and any investment and business activity, all of which are concentrated at the top. Comparing the loss with a forecast error, which has nothing to do with emigration, invites readers to call the loss trivial. The opening example, 1,000 leavers, is small.
- **Neutral choice:** Say in the lead that only Income Tax is counted and that the true loss would be larger. Drop the forecast-error yardstick, or pair it with one that cuts the other way, such as the loss compared with a named spending line. Show the 1,000 and 10,000 cases together in the lead.

## 6. `immigration.html`: the main concerns of migration sceptics are absent, and the page ends on a positive

- **Words:** The page says it covers "public money, public services and the economy". Its gap list begins: "How many NHS and adult social care staff are non-UK nationals." The last evidence box ends: "while migration still improves the public finances it does so by less … borrowing £13.1 billion lower".
- **What a newcomer takes from it:** Taken in this order, migration's fiscal effect is positive, and the next figure worth adding is how many migrants staff the NHS, which is a standard pro-migration point. Asylum, small-boat arrivals and asylum accommodation costs, which official Home Office statistics measure, are not mentioned anywhere on the page. Neither is housing demand. The "how much could the answer change" box quotes only the higher-migration scenario, not the lower one.
- **Neutral choice:** Include the official asylum and asylum-support cost figures, quote both OBR scenarios, and order the gaps neutrally, or list alongside NHS staffing a gap that sceptics would raise, such as housing demand.

## 7. `index.html`: the defence link presupposes the answer

- **Words:** The link to the defence page reads "Why can't we afford to spend more on defence?" The page itself is titled "What limits UK defence spending, and what would more cost?"
- **What a newcomer takes from it:** It is established that the UK cannot afford more defence, which suits the side of the argument that resists higher military spending and its trade-offs against social spending. The defence page then reinforces the point with "the second highest of the 6 G7 countries", on a measure that excludes military pensions and is lower than NATO's.
- **Neutral choice:** Use the page's own neutral title on the home page. Show the NATO-definition figure (2.32% for 2025 appears on the manifesto page) alongside the OECD one.

## 8. `welfare.html`: the series stops before the recent rise, and the lead stresses pensioners

- **Words:** "The figures stop at 2021-22." The lead says: "Pensioners received 54% of social security in 2021-22, against 59% in 1978-79." The charts describe non-pensioner spending as having "fell to £128bn in 2019-20".
- **What a newcomer takes from it:** Welfare is mostly pensions, working-age spending was cut in the 2010s, and nothing notable has happened since. The most debated change, the post-2021 rise in working-age incapacity and disability benefits, falls outside the window, so the page shows the austerity-era fall but not the recent rise. Pensioners' falling share is the lead's comparison, not working-age growth.
- **Neutral choice:** Add the DWP benefit expenditure tables or the OBR's forecast years to 2025-26 (the page already admits they are missing). Until then, say in the lead that the series ends before the recent rise in working-age health-related benefits.

## 9. `index.html`: the Who pays card adds a left-leaning rebuttal to its own headline

- **Words:** "The top 1% of Income Tax payers owed 27.2% of all Income Tax … They had 12.4% of all Income Tax payers' income. Every household also pays taxes on spending, such as VAT."
- **What a newcomer takes from it:** The top-earner figure is put in context twice in the same card, by income share and then by the reminder that everyone pays VAT. The next card then shows the poorest fifth paying the highest share (example 4). Together, the home page steers the reader away from "the rich pay most of the tax" and towards "everyone pays, and the poor pay proportionately more". The richest fifth's 46% share of all household taxes, which is on `who-pays.html`, does not appear on the home page.
- **Neutral choice:** Show the top-1% figure with its income share and nothing more, or add the "46% paid by the richest fifth" figure on the home page beside the VAT line.

## 10. `productivity.html` (and `spending.html`): 2008 baselines frame the 2010s as a decade of stagnation

- **Words:** "After inflation, UK output per hour rose by 3.9% between 2008 and 2021. The other five G7 countries on this page rose by 10.3%", with the chart rebased to 2008 = 100. On `spending.html`, the chart description singles out "little change from 2010-11 (£1,116bn) to 2019-20 (£1,132bn)".
- **What a newcomer takes from it:** The years of Conservative government, 2010 to 2021, were a lost decade of flat productivity and flat spending, which supports the "austerity failed" story. Starting at 2008, a financial-crisis peak that hit the UK's large financial sector especially hard, makes the UK's growth look as weak as possible. The ONS series starts in 1997, which the page mentions but does not chart.
- **Neutral choice:** Rebase to the start of the ONS series (1997), or show both, so the reader can see whether the gap opened before or after 2008. On the spending page, describe the after-inflation series by its first and last years only, as the methods page says comparisons should be made.

---

That is ten examples. Numbers 1 to 3 are the strongest. Numbers 7 to 10 are softer framing points rather than wrong figures.
