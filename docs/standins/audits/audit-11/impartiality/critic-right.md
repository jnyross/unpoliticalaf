# Critic: the case that the site leans right

Scope: the built pages `site/index.html`, `spending.html`, `taxes.html`,
`who-pays.html`, `household-tax.html`, `life/index.html` and `methods.html`
(site version b08069b). I read no code and no other review.

A fair note first: the site is careful. Almost every example below comes with
a caveat somewhere on the page. The case rests on what a newcomer meets first,
such as headlines, chart titles, lead sentences and default comparisons. These
come before the caveats, and they repeatedly frame things in ways a
right-of-centre reader would choose. Ten examples follow, strongest first.

---

## 1. "Who pays?" opens with the top 1%'s share of Income Tax

**Pages:** `index.html` ("Who pays?" panel); `who-pays.html` (headline and lead).

**Exact words:** Big figure "**27.2%** of all Income Tax was paid by the top 1%
of Income Tax payers". Lead sentence on who-pays: "**Higher-income people pay
most Income Tax.**" The section 1 bar chart, "Income Tax, by person", shows
only shares of tax: Top 1% 27.2%, Top 10% 59.1%, Top 50% 90.1%, Bottom 50%
9.9%. Their shares of income (12.4%, 34.0%, 75.1%, 24.9%) appear only in the
table, which is folded away.

**What a newcomer takes from it:** the rich already carry the system, so
taxing them more would be unfair or pointless. This is the opening move of
"the rich pay their fair share" arguments, such as those of the Taxpayers'
Alliance and the CPS. The measure counts Income Tax only. It also excludes
everyone who owes no Income Tax ("People who owe no Income Tax are not
included"), and that makes the top shares look larger. Income Tax is the most
progressive tax, so leading with it also shows the system at its most
progressive.

**Neutral choice:** lead "Who pays?" with all taxes against income, by fifth.
The site has this figure on household-tax: 36.7% for the lowest fifth and
36.2% for the highest. Or plot share of income and share of tax side by side
in the visible chart, and keep the sentence descriptive, without "pay most".

## 2. Debt, borrowing and spending turned into a bill per person and per household

**Pages:** `taxes.html` ("Borrowing is not the same as debt" and the "For
scale" note); `spending.html` ("For scale"); also the per-household column in
the spending table.

**Exact words:** "Debt … £2,917bn … about **£41,978 per person (£100,569 per
household)**." "Borrowing was **£1,933 per person, or £4,630 per household**."
"spending … about **£46,895 per household, or £902 a week**." Spending table
column "£ per household": "Social protection … **£14,043**".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** "every household owes £100,000" and "the
state spends £900 a week on my household". This is the household-budget
framing of the national debt that austerity arguments rely on. The caveat ("it
is owed by the public sector, not by people or households") comes after the
figures and is weaker than them.

**Neutral choice:** keep debt as a share of GDP, which the site already gives
(93.3%). Drop the per-household debt and borrowing figures, or set them beside
equivalent per-household assets or GDP per household so the scale has
something to compare with.

## 3. "Who receives more in benefits than they pay in taxes?"

**Page:** `who-pays.html`, section 4.

**Exact words:** "In 2023/24, **53.3% of people** lived in households that
received more in benefits and services than they paid in taxes." The chart
title adds that it ran "in every year from 1977".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** most people are net takers and a minority
of net contributors carries them. This is the "makers and takers" frame. The
topic gets a whole section with a question heading. Benefits here include the
value of the NHS and state schools, so on this measure a child in a state
school counts as "receiving more". The lifetime caveat ("the same person can
be on either side at different times") sits under a fold.

**Neutral choice:** leave the section out of a "who pays" page, or frame it
over the life cycle (ONS and IFS lifetime analysis), or at least lead with the
lifetime caveat rather than the single-year headline.

## 4. The ten-year spending window starts after the austerity cuts

**Page:** `spending.html`, "Change by area over ten years: 2015-16 to 2025-26".

**Exact words:** "The two years are a fixed ten years apart, not chosen for
the result." Every area except one shows a "rise": Education 7% rise, Public
order and safety 33% rise, Housing 64% rise, and so on. Only "Recreation,
culture and religion -4% fall".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** spending has gone up on almost everything,
so there were no real cuts. The window starts in 2015-16, after the 2010–2015
squeeze. Area-by-area cuts after 2009-10 never appear in a headline view; a
reader must open a separate page. The figures are real totals, not per head,
and the UK population grew over the decade. So "Education 7% rise" can hide
flat or falling spending per pupil.

**Neutral choice:** show the longest comparable window (2003-04 to 2025-26),
or two windows (2009-10 to 2015-16 and 2015-16 to 2025-26), and show per-head
figures beside the totals.

## 5. Earnings comparison that hides the lost decade of real pay

**Pages:** `index.html` and `life/index.html` (Earnings).

**Exact words:** "April 2021 £761 → April 2025 £767 … **about the same**
(+0.8%)." "The dark dot is 2025: £767, **the highest of the 5 years measured
the same way**." The scale note shows that the top of the range is 2008:
"from £717 (2014) to £771 (2008)".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** pay is at a high and holding up. The
coding-break rule ("pay is compared only within each stretch") means the page
never says that real median pay in 2025 is still at or below 2008. The
fourteen-year real-pay stagnation over the Coalition and Conservative years is
a central left-of-centre argument, and here it is left unsaid.

**Neutral choice:** state the 2008 and 2025 figures side by side, with the
coding change noted, as the small chart already does. Don't attach "the
highest" to a five-year stretch.

## 6. The suggested default comparison is 2011 against 2019

**Page:** `life/index.html`, "Compare two years".

**Exact words:** "Start with **2011 compared with 2019** … 2011 came soon after
a recession and 2019 was the last year before the pandemic."

**What a newcomer takes from it:** the one comparison the site suggests runs
over exactly the Coalition and Conservative years, from a post-recession low
to a pre-pandemic peak. Employment rose to its 2019 high (76.1%) over that
span. The page sets up a flattering before-and-after for one period of
government. The reason given is data coverage, but the added commentary on why
the two years are good endpoints ("soon after a recession", "last year before
the pandemic") is an editorial framing of the cycle.

**Neutral choice:** suggest the earliest and latest common years with no
commentary on the economic cycle, or offer no default pair.

## 7. Debt interest: the larger gross measure, mentioned again and again

**Pages:** `index.html`, `spending.html`.

**Exact words:** Homepage: "Debt interest (£11) is inside general public
services", plus a bar label "(incl. debt interest)". Spending page: "interest
on government debt (£11 of every £100 spent on services …)". It names the
measure ("this counts interest paid out") and notes that the OBR and IFS
"net" figure "is lower". A fold-out explains why debt interest rose in 2022-23.

**What a newcomer takes from it:** debt is already eating a large part of the
budget, so the state must borrow less. Of the two measures, the site picks the
larger (gross). It mentions debt interest on the homepage although debt
interest is not one of the COFOG areas shown. Other sub-items get no such
attention, apart from a passing line on transport.

**Neutral choice:** use the net measure the OBR uses, or show both equally.
Treat debt interest no differently from other sub-items such as schools or
the State Pension.

## 8. An old, out-of-date ONS quote that explains away the indirect-tax burden on poorer households

**Page:** `household-tax.html`, fold-out "Why can indirect taxes be a large
share of income for lower-income households?" and the table "Why the same tax
can look bigger or smaller".

**Exact words:** It quotes ONS: "Some households … may have annual expenditure
that exceeds their annual income … funding their expenditure from savings or
borrowing, thereby adjusting their lifetime consumption." The page itself says
"This is from the ONS bulletin for April 2015 to March 2016 … the latest ONS
bulletin does not repeat this explanation." The table follows: indirect taxes
for the lowest fifth are "21.0% of gross income … 16.5% of spending", against
"15.4%" for the highest fifth.

**What a newcomer takes from it:** VAT and duties are not really regressive.
The poor only look heavily taxed because they draw on savings, and as a share
of spending everyone pays about the same. This is the standard right-leaning
reply to "VAT hits the poor hardest". The site goes back nine years for a
passage the ONS has since dropped, and only for this one argument.

**Neutral choice:** quote only the current bulletin, or give no explanation.
If the share-of-spending view stays, keep it as a plain table without a
heading that suggests the burden is an illusion.

## 9. "Tax burden" wording, rising tax forecasts, and quotes about the threshold freeze

**Pages:** `taxes.html`; `household-tax.html`.

**Exact words:** "Taxes alone were 36.0% of GDP in 2025-26, a share news
reports often call the **'tax burden'**." Chart description: receipts "rose to
40.1% in 2025-26 … **Forecast for 2031-32: 42.9%**", close to the post-war
record of 43.3% printed in the same line. The quoted HMRC text runs: "frozen
Income Tax thresholds … due to the extension of the freeze at **Autumn Budget
2025**". The quoted ONS text runs: "creating **fiscal drag** and so
increasing households tax liabilities … pushed into higher tax brackets".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** taxes are at near-record highs and rising,
and the current government's freeze is pulling people into higher bands.
"Tax burden" is a loaded term. Putting it in quotation marks still teaches it
as the name for the figure. Two of the few causal explanations on the tax
pages are about stealth tax rises. None is about, for example, why receipts
fell in the 1990s.

**Neutral choice:** say "tax as a share of GDP" without introducing "tax
burden". Show forecasts without setting them next to historical highs. Choose
explanatory quotes in proportion across the series, not only for recent
rises.

## 10. Spending growth emphasised by the lead sentence and a truncated axis

**Page:** `spending.html`, "How has spending changed since 2003-04?"

**Exact words:** Lead: "After adjusting for rising prices, total spending in
2025-26 was **54% higher** than in 2003-04." The share-of-GDP chart: "The scale
starts at **35**, not at zero." The headline cards: "**£33** of every £100
spent on services went on social protection, the largest area".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** the state has grown sharply and welfare is
its biggest item. The figure that leads is the real-terms total, the most
dramatic measure: it is not per head and not a share of GDP. A line chart
starting at 35% makes the rise from 38.7% to 44.3% look steep. The headline
names social protection with "(pensions, benefits, social care)" and its
per-household cost, which pushes the reader towards "welfare is the problem".
The site does not mention that most of social protection is the State
Pension; its own figure for old-age pensions is £155bn of £407bn.

**Neutral choice:** lead with the share of GDP or real spending per person.
Start the share-of-GDP axis at zero, as the bars do. In the headline, say that
pensions are the largest part of social protection.

---

**How strong is the case?** The strongest items are 1–5. They are first-screen
choices: headlines, default views and comparison windows, and a newcomer meets
them before any caveat. Items 6–10 are real but smaller. Most are framing or
emphasis, and the site's own caveats partly undo them. I found ten examples,
but several are matters of degree rather than clear tilts. On some of them a
critic from the left could fairly say the opposite. For example, the
household-tax page puts the lowest fifth's 36.7% beside the highest fifth's
36.2%, which on its own reads as the poor being taxed as heavily as the rich.
