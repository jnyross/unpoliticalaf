# Critic (left lean): audit 11

Brief: make the strongest honest case that the built site leans left in UK politics. I read the built HTML in `site/` only: index, spending, taxes, who-pays (plus who-pays-age and who-pays-households), household-tax, life/index (plus life/housing, earnings, house-prices, holidays and the 2008 to 2017 year pages), international, methods and glossary. I did not read the code or any other review.

Overall: the site is careful, and the lean is weak. Many of its choices push the other way. The top 1% Income Tax share leads "Who pays". Debt interest is shown gross, which gives the larger figure. The spending page leads with "54% higher than in 2003-04" after inflation. The household-tax page quotes ONS on fiscal drag. Section 4 of who-pays reports that 53.3% of people are net recipients. The ten items below are the best left-leaning case I can honestly make. Items 1 to 5 are real framing choices. Items 6 to 10 are weaker, and a fair reader could dismiss them.

---

## 1. Headline "share of income in tax" makes the tax system look flat or regressive

**Page:** `index.html` ("How much household income goes on tax?" card) and `household-tax.html` (hero and first section).

**Exact words:** "33.6% of gross household income … Lowest-income fifth 36.7% … Highest-income fifth 36.2%"; "The share was similar for the lowest-income fifth of people ( 36.7% ) and the highest-income fifth ( 36.2% )"; "For the lowest-income fifth, most of this is indirect tax such as VAT ( 21.0% of income)". The section chart is titled "Taxes took a similar share of income for the lowest-income fifth and the highest-income fifth in 2023/24".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** the poorest pay as large a share of their income in tax as the richest, so UK taxes are not progressive and VAT hits the poor hardest. This is a standard left-of-centre argument, used for example by inequality campaigners against VAT rises.

**Why it's a choice:** the denominator is gross income, and that choice gives this result. The site's own table, further down the page, shows that indirect tax as a share of *spending* is 16.5% for the lowest fifth and 15.4% for the highest, which is almost flat. The ONS caveat that low-income households spend more than their income sits inside a collapsed "Why can…" box, quoted from a 2017 bulletin. The homepage card adds no caveat at all.

**A neutral choice:** give equal weight in the headline to pounds paid (£8,625 against £51,903, on the who-pays page) or to the share of spending, next to the share of gross income. Or put the "spending can exceed income" caveat on the homepage card itself.

## 2. A caveat is attached to the "rich pay most" fact, but not to the "poor pay as much" fact

**Page:** `index.html`, "Who pays?" card.

**Exact words:** "27.2% of all Income Tax was paid by the top 1% … They had 12.4% of all Income Tax payers' income. **Every household also pays taxes on spending, such as VAT.**"

**What a newcomer takes from it:** the top-1% figure comes with a rebuttal ("don't forget, everyone pays tax"). The next card ("lowest-income fifth 36.7%") comes with no matching counterweight, such as "the highest fifth paid six times as much in pounds".

**A neutral choice:** either remove the rider from the top-1% card, or give each card a matching rider. For example, on the tax-and-income card: "In pounds, the highest-income fifth's households paid £51,903 on average, the lowest £8,625."

## 3. "Tax burden" in scare quotes, with a disclaimer that defuses it

**Pages:** `taxes.html` ("Receipts and spending since 1948-49"); `glossary.html`.

**Exact words:** "Taxes alone were 36.0% of GDP in 2025-26, a share news reports often call the 'tax burden'. … so it is not anyone's tax bill." The glossary says: "This site does not use the name itself … when this share goes up, it does not mean everyone pays more, and when it goes down, it does not mean everyone pays less."

**What a newcomer takes from it:** concern about a high or rising tax burden, a central centre-right argument, is a media framing that should be distrusted. The disclaimer is technically true, but no similar distancing language is used for terms the left favours. "Fiscal drag" and "pay freeze" are quoted without comment (see item 5). The taxes page also never says plainly that receipts are forecast to reach 42.9% of GDP, near the post-war high of 43.3%. That fact appears only inside the chart's long text description ("In between: highest 43.3% (1949-50)").

**A neutral choice:** use the term without quote marks as a common name, with one line of definition. And state in the lede where 2025-26 and the forecast sit in the post-war range.

## 4. Reassurance that public debt "is owed by the public sector, not by people"

**Page:** `taxes.html`, "Borrowing is not the same as debt".

**Exact words:** "£2,917bn … about £41,978 per person ( £100,569 per household). That is the debt divided by everyone, an average, not anyone's bill: **it is owed by the public sector, not by people or households.**"

**What a newcomer takes from it:** the national debt is not really their problem. That supports a relaxed view of borrowing, which is closer to the left's position than the right's. Taxpayers ultimately service the debt, and the site's own spending page counts £11 of every £100 on interest. The sentence closes off that link.

**A neutral choice:** stop at "an average, not anyone's bill". Or add the balancing fact: "interest on it is paid from public spending (see spending)".

## 5. Government policy named as the cause of pay stagnation, on ten year pages

**Pages:** `life/2008.html` to `life/2017.html` (earnings section); the earnings sparkline on `index.html` and `life/index.html`.

**Exact words:** "Why did pay fall behind prices after 2008? … 'In the public sector, a pay freeze (from 2011) and pay cap (from 2013) kept wage rises below inflation, while in the private sector wage growth was also slow.'"

**What a newcomer takes from it:** falling real pay in the 2010s was partly caused by Coalition and Conservative policy. This is the only causal explanation offered for pay, and it is repeated on every year page from 2008 to 2017. It even appears on 2008 to 2010, before the freeze existed. The site's own principle is "Change is not cause". Quoting ONS gets round that principle, but choosing *which* ONS sentence to quote is still a choice. The other ONS factors (productivity, the recession itself) are not given equal space. Separately, the sparkline says "The scale runs from £717 (2014) to £771 (2008) and does not start at zero". That draws the eye to a 2014 trough which, on a zero-based scale, would be a 7% dip.

**A neutral choice:** show the quote only on 2011 to 2017, or pick an ONS passage on the overall causes. Or give each period's year pages a "why" quote of equal weight. For example, 2021 to 2023 also saw real pay fall, and those pages have no comparable policy-naming quote.

## 6. Housing compared with the low point of private renting

**Pages:** `life/index.html`, `life/housing.html`, `life/house-prices.html`.

**Exact words:** "In 2024-25, 65% … 19% rented privately. … **In 2008-09, 14% rented privately.**" Also: "the home cost 7.55 times a year's earnings, compared with 3.89 times in 1999".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** a steadily worsening "generation rent" or housing crisis. 2008-09 is the lowest point in the series shown (the sparkline caption gives 14.2% in 2008-09 as its minimum). Private renting peaked at 20.3% in 2016-17 and has fallen since, but that only appears in the chart's text description, not in the lede. The house-price lede does mention the 2021 peak, which is fairer.

**A neutral choice:** a lede of the form "rose from 14% in 2008-09 to a peak of 20% in 2016-17, and was 19% in 2024-25".

## 7. "UK tax is close to the OECD average": the comparison set and measure flatter the UK

**Page:** `international.html` (lede and G7 section).

**Exact words:** "In 2024, UK taxes were 34.4% of GDP, close to the OECD average of 34.1%"; "the UK had the second lowest tax as a share of GDP in 2024 (of the 6 with a figure for that year)".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** the UK is a low-to-average tax country, so there is room to raise taxes, which is a left-of-centre conclusion. The average is a simple unweighted mean that includes Mexico, Colombia, Costa Rica and others. "Second lowest in the G7" is driven by the fact that only the US is lower, and Japan is missing. The OECD figure (34.4%) is also lower than the site's own headline measure (36.0% of GDP on the taxes page). The page does not explain that gap.

**A neutral choice:** give the UK's rank against a peer group of similar income alongside the OECD average, and in the same lede note its position relative to its own history. Explain why 34.4% here and 36.0% on the taxes page differ.

## 8. Welfare spending framed as mainly pensions; working-age benefits never given a figure

**Page:** `spending.html`.

**Exact words:** "the largest share, £33, went on social protection: mainly State Pension, other benefits and social care"; label "Social protection (pensions, benefits, social care)"; "Pensions in old age cost £155bn in 2025-26".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** the biggest spending area is mostly pensions. Pensions are £155bn of £407bn, about 38%, so "mainly State Pension" overstates the case. Pensions are the one subtotal broken out. Working-age and incapacity benefits, the subtotal the right most often cites, are never given a figure.

**A neutral choice:** say "including", not "mainly", and break out working-age benefits from the same PESA table as pensions.

## 9. Deficits presented as the historical norm

**Page:** `taxes.html`, chart headline in "Receipts and spending since 1948-49".

**Exact words:** "Spending was higher than receipts in 68 of the 78 years from 1948-49 to 2025-26" (and "65 of the 71 years" in the after-inflation view).

**What a newcomer takes from it:** borrowing is normal and nothing to worry about. The headline does not mention debt rising from 35.5% of GDP in 2007-08 to 93.3%, although that figure is in the table on the same page.

**A neutral choice:** a headline that describes the two lines' levels and turning points, as the spending page does, rather than a count that implies a norm. Or pair it with the debt trajectory.

## 10. "Gap between richer and poorer" framing on the tax-and-income page

**Page:** `household-tax.html`, collapsed box "What do taxes and benefits do to the gap between richer and poorer households?"

**Exact words:** ONS quote: "The richest fifth … 12.2 times larger than the poorest fifth … reduced to 3.3 times".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** the page's frame is inequality between rich and poor, which is a left-coded lens. This is weak: the quote itself arguably shows heavy redistribution, which the right could also use. I include it because it is the only "why" box on the page that is framed around a distributional gap.

**A neutral choice:** title it with what the quote measures, for example "How do taxes and benefits change incomes across the fifths?".

---

**Not counted, and why:** the class-size sparklines on non-zero scales ("26.2 (2008) to 27.1 (2016)") exaggerate small moves, but the site says so and it is not clearly partisan. The life page's default comparison of 2011 against 2019 spans a Conservative-led period, but the outcome mostly flatters it (employment up), so it is not a left lean. The 2015-16 to 2025-26 spending window shows real rises almost everywhere, which cuts against an "austerity" story.
