# Impartiality judgement: audit 11

I checked each critic's examples against the built pages in `site/` (version b08069b)
and the rule in `docs/standins/impartiality-rule.md`. I did not read the builder code,
the goal scorecard or any earlier audit. "Material" uses the rule's own test: a newcomer
would come away with a picture of the facts, or a sense of which side is right, that the
official evidence does not support.

Summary: 20 examples. 0 upheld as material, 3 upheld as minor, 17 rejected. Six of the
examples are the same choice criticised from both sides: the tax share by fifth (L1/R8),
"tax burden" (L3/R9), debt per household (L4/R2), the social protection headline (L8/R10),
the top 1% lead (L2/R1) and the 2011 against 2019 pair (R6 and the left critic's note).
In most of these the page gives both views.

## Breaches the critics missed

I checked start years, chart scales and group order on index, spending, spending-areas,
spending-history, taxes, tax-history, who-pays (and its age and household pages),
household-tax, international, borrowing, methods, glossary and life (index, work,
earnings, housing, house-prices, holidays, schools, and sample year pages). Bars start
at zero: the HTML bar widths are proportional to their values. Every line chart that
starts above zero says so in its caption. Small-multiple panels say they share a scale,
and their axes do. Fifths, age bands, nations and regions are in official order, and
rankings are labelled as rankings.

1. **Material: a change worked out across a break (life/holidays.html).** The chart title
   says "Holiday visits abroad by UK residents rose until 2019, fell in 2021, then rose
   back to about the 2019 level". The 2025 figure (58.4 million) comes from the fully new
   method and covers Great Britain residents. The 2019 figure (58.7 million) covers UK
   residents on the old method. The chart's own text says "values either side are not
   compared". On a comparable basis the latest figure is 2023 at 55.5 million, below 2019.
   The title tells a newcomer that holidays have recovered, which the official series
   cannot show. It should describe each stretch separately, as the chart text does.
2. **Minor: headline not for the latest measured year (life/index.html, life/work.html).**
   Work leads with April to June 2025 (75.3%), but the homepage shows April to June 2026
   (75.1%). The schools page says "the other topics have no 2026 figures yet", which
   contradicts the homepage.
3. **Minor: comparisons start in 2008, not at the first year of the series (life section).**
   The class sizes lede compares January 2025 with January 2008 (secondary 20.9 to 22.5).
   The same series runs from 2006 with no break, when secondary was 21.5. The work chart
   title uses 2008 although the series starts in 1971. The 2008 window is applied evenly,
   so it does not look chosen for the result, but it is not the rule's "first and latest
   year".
4. **Minor: two years set side by side across source breaks (spending-history.html).** The
   lede puts 1955-56 (IFS figures) next to 2021-22 (PESA from 2020-21). The page's own
   chart says each side of those breaks "is read separately". It also says the mix "has
   changed a great deal", which judges the figures rather than describing them.
5. **Minor: own-words emphasis and cause (borrowing.html).** "The UK's interest bill rose
   sharply in 2022" is followed at once, in the site's own words, by index-linked gilts as
   the mechanism. The Limits box also says, in the site's own words, that euro-area yield
   gaps "say more about how investors see each government". The rule asks for neutral
   verbs, and for any cause to be an attributed quote.
