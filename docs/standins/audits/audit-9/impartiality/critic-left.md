# Critic (left lean): the case that the site nudges newcomers leftwards

Brief: make the strongest honest case that the built site leans left in UK
politics. I read only the built HTML pages (home, spending, spending-areas,
spending-history, taxes, tax-history, who-pays, who-pays-age,
who-pays-households, household-tax, international, life/index, life/earnings,
life/housing, life/work, life/compare/2009-2023, glossary, methods). I read no
code, docs, builder, scripts or reviews, and did not use the web.

Overall: the site is careful. Most wording is flat, and it often adds context
that cuts against the left (for example, the top 1%'s share of income beside
their share of Income Tax, and the bottom-tenth pay line rising 17% after
inflation while the top-tenth line fell 4%). The examples below are real, but
several are about emphasis rather than error. I found ten worth listing. The
last three are noticeably weaker than the first five.

---

## 1. "Taxes took the largest share of income for the lowest-income fifth"

**Page:** `household-tax.html` (chart title on the 1977 to 2023/24 panels), and
the "How much household income goes on tax?" card on `index.html`.

**Exact words / chart:**
- Chart title: "Taxes took the largest share of income for the lowest-income fifth in 2023/24".
- Lede: "The share was similar for the lowest-income fifth of people (36.7%) and the highest-income fifth (36.2%), but for different reasons."
- Home card: a list that opens with "Lowest-income fifth 36.7%" and ends with "Highest-income fifth 36.2%", with nothing to say that each side is only one fifth of the picture.

**What a newcomer takes from it:** the poorest pay the highest share of their
income in tax, so the tax system is regressive or at best flat. "The poor pay
more tax than the rich" is a familiar left campaigning line.

**Why it leans:** the chart title calls the bottom fifth "the largest" on a
0.5-point gap between two survey estimates. On the same page, the ONS warns
that lump-sum deferred tax payments "can make direct taxes look high for the
lowest-income fifths", but that warning sits in the Limits text, not beside
the headline. The bottom fifth's denominator is gross income, while their
spending is "some of it from savings or borrowing". That is mentioned once and
not on the home page. The ONS's own net position (after cash benefits and
services) shows the bottom fifth as large net recipients. The site has this
figure (who-pays §4) but does not put it next to this chart.

**Neutral choice:** a chart title like "Taxes took a similar share of gross
income for the lowest and highest fifths". Put the ONS deferred-payment warning
next to the 36.7% figure. On the home card, add the disposable-income or
"after benefits" view, or link straight to it.

## 2. The only official "why" for flat pay names a government's policy

**Page:** `life/earnings.html`, "What official bodies say about these figures".

**Exact words:** "Why did pay fall behind prices after 2008?", answered with the
ONS: "In the public sector, a pay freeze (from 2011) and pay cap (from 2013)
kept wage rises below inflation, while in the private sector wage growth was
also slow."

**What a newcomer takes from it:** real pay stagnated because of the
2010-onwards austerity policy. This is the standard left story of the 2010s.

**Why it leans:** the site's rule is "Change is not cause" and "It does not say
why in its own words". Choosing which official explanation to quote is still
an editorial act. This is the only causal account of pay on the page, and the
cause it names is a specific government's policy. Productivity, the financial
crisis, sterling's fall and the 2022 energy shock are not quoted. The page
does quote ONS on 2022 real pay, but only to give the size of the fall, not its
cause.

**Neutral choice:** quote a wider ONS or OBR passage that lists several causes,
led by the recession and productivity, or quote nothing causal. If the
pay-freeze quote stays, give it equal space alongside an explanation that does
not blame policy.

## 3. Pay measured from its pre-crash peak, on a zoomed-in scale

**Pages:** `index.html` (Earnings card), `life/index.html`, `life/earnings.html`.

**Exact words / chart:** "In April 2025, the middle full-time employee earned
£767 a week before tax. After allowing for rising prices, that was about the
same as in April 2008, which was £771 in April 2025 prices." The sparkline
caption: "The scale runs from £717 (2014) to £771 (2008) and does not start at
zero."

**What a newcomer takes from it:** "a lost 17 years for wages". The zoomed
scale, from £717 to £771, turns a 7% range into a deep valley.

**Why it leans:** 2008 is the highest point in the whole series (the chart
text says "highest £771 (April 2008)"). Measuring from a peak is the most
pessimistic choice available. The site says 2008 is used because every topic
has data from then, which is a defensible reason, but the effect falls on one
side. The headline also compares 2008 with 2025 across the 2011 and 2021 coding
breaks, while methods.html says "no change is calculated across the break". On
`life/earnings.html`, the 2006 to 2025 change (+1.5%) is shown, but only below
the fold.

**Neutral choice:** lead with the change since the last method break (2021 to
2025, which is a rise), or give two windows side by side. Draw the small chart
on a scale that includes a reference band, or state the percentage range in
the caption.

## 4. The suggested comparison is "2009 compared with 2023", a pay trough

**Page:** `life/index.html` ("Start with 2009 compared with 2023") and
`life/compare/2009-2023.html`.

**Exact words:** "Start with 2009 compared with 2023, the earliest and latest
years in which all five topics can be compared". On the compare page: "Median
full-time weekly pay, after inflation, went down from £769 (April 2009) to £737
(April 2023). Change: -4.1%." Under "Largest changes": "Families with dependent
children renting privately, 16.8% → 24.4%" and "Median house price ÷ median
full-time earnings … 6.35 times → 8.28 times (+30%)".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** pay is down, renting is up and homes are
less affordable. Everyday life has got worse since the Labour-to-Coalition
handover.

**Why it leans:** 2023 is the latest low in real pay (£737). By 2025 pay is
£767. The one comparison the site recommends by default lands on the bleakest
recent year for the headline living-standards measure. The reason given
(all five topics) is a technical one, but newcomers who follow the suggestion
get the gloomiest picture.

**Neutral choice:** suggest no default pair, or suggest the latest year
against a fixed round baseline. Alternatively, show two suggested pairs, one
ending in the latest year.

## 5. Distancing from the "tax burden" and never saying it is historically high

**Pages:** `taxes.html` (Receipts and spending since 1948-49), `glossary.html`
("Tax burden"), `tax-history.html`.

**Exact words:**
- taxes.html: "Taxes alone were 36.0% of GDP in 2025-26, a share news reports often call the “tax burden”. It compares all tax, paid by people and businesses, with the size of the economy, so it is not anyone's tax bill".
- Glossary: "This site does not use the name itself … when this share goes up, it does not mean everyone pays more".
- Chart description: receipts "rose to 40.1% in 2025-26. In between: highest 43.3% (1949-50)".
- tax-history.html: "All public sector receipts were 42.9% of GDP in 1948-49 and 40.3% in 2022-23."

**What a newcomer takes from it:** the "tax burden" is a loaded media phrase
that doesn't mean much, and taxes today are below the post-war level.

**Why it leans:** a high and rising tax take is a central argument on the
right. The site adds caveats to the term only when it is mentioned. It never
states the plain fact its own table supports: the share is the highest in
decades, and OBR forecasts take receipts to 42.9% in 2031-32, the 1948-49 level.
The long chart uses all receipts rather than taxes alone, and the receipts
series has its peak in 1949-50. A newcomer therefore reads "highest 43.3%
(1949-50)" and concludes that today is not unusual. The caveat that "when this
share goes up, it does not mean everyone pays more" has no matching caveat for
spending as a share of GDP.

**Neutral choice:** state the rank plainly, for example "the highest share
since …" as the OBR does, using taxes alone. Then give the caveat once, in
neutral terms, and apply the same caveat to the spending share.

## 6. The comparator for "UK taxes" is an OECD average that includes Mexico and Colombia

**Pages:** `international.html`, plus the link from `index.html`.

**Exact words:** "In 2024, UK taxes were 34.4% of GDP, close to the OECD average
of 34.1%." "Of the 36 countries with a figure for 2024, 21 collected a larger
share of GDP in tax than the UK." The bar list opens with Denmark 45.2%, France
43.5% and Austria 43.4%.

**What a newcomer takes from it:** the UK is a middling or low-tax country with
room to raise more, as the Nordic and continental states do.

**Why it leans:** the OECD simple average counts Mexico (18.3%), Colombia,
Chile, Costa Rica and Turkey equally with Germany. Leading with "21 collected a
larger share" counts countries rather than measuring distance, and the list is
topped by high-tax European states. Common alternatives such as the G7, or the
UK's own history (see example 5), would put the UK nearer the top. The spending
side is framed more gently: "above the average of 44.5%".

**Neutral choice:** add a G7 or population-weighted comparison, or rank
without the "how many collected more" sentence. Lead tax and spending with
parallel wording, such as "X points above/below".

## 7. A VAT rider attached to the top-1% fact

**Page:** `index.html` ("Who pays?" card); a similar move in the `who-pays.html` lede.

**Exact words:** "27.2% of all Income Tax was paid by the top 1% of Income Tax
payers in 2023 to 2024. They had 12.4% of all Income Tax payers' income. Every
household also pays taxes on spending, such as VAT."

**What a newcomer takes from it:** the top-1% figure is misleading, because
everyone pays tax and the poor pay through VAT. The closing sentence works as a
rebuttal.

**Why it leans:** no other card on the home page ends with a "but also"
sentence that undercuts its own headline. For example, the spending card has no
"but borrowing funds part of this". Putting the income share right beside the
tax share is fair context. Adding the VAT rider as well tilts the card further
towards the reading "the rich don't really pay that much".

**Neutral choice:** keep the income share and drop the VAT sentence, or move it
to the household-taxes card, where it belongs.

## 8. Housing: emphasis and quotes chosen for renter hardship

**Pages:** `life/index.html`, `life/housing.html`.

**Exact words / charts:**
- The life index sparkline for Housing tracks "Households renting privately" (rising). The home page uses ownership for the same topic.
- The housing lede ends with the extra clause "In 2008-09, 14% rented privately."
- It places "The average private rent was £250 a week … and the average mortgage payment £242" side by side.
- All four official quotes deal with strain: the private rented sector "has doubled in size"; "71% of private renters in the lowest two income quintiles spent more than 30% of their gross household income on rent"; "first time buyers with a mortgage of 30 years or more has increased significantly"; and outright owners rising because of "population ageing".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** "Generation Rent": housing has failed young
and low-income people, and renters are squeezed.

**Why it leans:** each figure is accurate. What leans is the choice of which
series to draw and which official sentences to quote. The same MHCLG quote
notes that private renting "has remained around 19% or 20% since 2013-14", but
the lede does not, and the sparkline starts at the 2008-09 low (14.2%). There
is no quote on, for example, rising outright ownership as a positive, or on
falling overcrowding among owners.

**Neutral choice:** use the same housing series on the home and life pages
(ownership). Mention in the lede that private renting has been stable since
2013-14. Balance the quotes, or reduce them to one description of the data.

## 9. "Spending was higher than receipts in 68 of the 78 years"

**Page:** `taxes.html`, chart title for Receipts and spending since 1948-49 (and
"65 of the 71 years" in the after-inflation view).

**What a newcomer takes from it:** deficits are normal and nearly permanent, so
today's £134bn of borrowing is nothing unusual. That view supports
"borrow to invest" and "don't worry about the deficit".

**Why it leans:** of all the things the 78-year chart shows, the headline picks
the one that normalises borrowing. Other choices were available: debt rising
from 25.6% of GDP (1988-89) to 93.3%; spending above 44% of GDP in every year
since 2021-22; or receipts at a multi-decade high. The table shows them, but
none is a headline.

**Neutral choice:** a descriptive title, such as "Receipts and spending as a
share of GDP, 1948-49 to 2025-26, with OBR forecasts", or a title that states
the latest gap and debt level.

## 10. "Why" boxes: spending rises put down to shocks, pay falls to policy

**Pages:** `spending.html` (three ONS quotes) compared with `life/earnings.html`
(example 2).

**Exact words:**
- On spending: "Why did spending jump in 2020-21?", answered with the pandemic and furlough.
- "Why did spending rise again in 2022-23?", answered with "rising inflation and energy costs".
- "Why did interest on government debt rise in 2022-23?", answered with "rises in the Retail Prices Index have increased the interest payable on index-linked gilts".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** higher public spending is forced on
governments by events, so it is not a choice to question. The debt-interest
bill is about inflation, not the size of the debt.

**Why it leans:** taken one by one, each quote is accurate and fair. Taken
together, they show a pattern. When spending goes up, the quoted cause is
outside anyone's control. When pay goes down, the quoted cause is a government
pay policy. There is no quote on spending choices: for example, the growth of
social protection, which rose £40bn in real terms over ten years, or of health.
The debt-interest quote also leaves out the stock of debt as a cause.

**Neutral choice:** the same rule for both. Either quote no causes, or for each
"why" pick the publisher's fullest list of causes, including policy decisions
where the publisher names them.

### Checked and not counted (they cut the other way or are neutral)

- who-pays §4, "53.3% of people lived in households that received more in benefits and services than they paid in taxes": if anything this leans right.
- Spending per head and per household ("£46,895 per household"), the spending share-of-GDP axis starting at 35%, and "£11 of every £100 went on interest": these lean towards fiscal concern rather than left.
- The ten-year spending window from 2015-16 leaves out the 2010-15 cuts. That is the opposite of a left-leaning choice.
- The pay-spread section reports the bottom tenth +17% and the top tenth −4% since 2008, against the "inequality is rising" story.
- The household-tax worked example (£30,000 employee, "16% of their pay") leaves out employer National Insurance and VAT. That understates the tax on an ordinary worker, but the site says so, and it is not clearly partisan.

Count: 10 (numbers 8 to 10 are weaker, emphasis-level points).
