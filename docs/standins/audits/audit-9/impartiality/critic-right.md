# Critic: the case that the site leans right

Scope: the built pages only (`index.html`, `spending.html`, `taxes.html`, `who-pays.html`, `who-pays-age.html`, `household-tax.html`, `international.html`, `life/index.html`, `life/compare/2009-2023.html`, `methods.html`). I did not read the code, docs or any review.

I found ten examples worth listing. Several are partly offset elsewhere on the site. Where they are, I say so: the argument is about what a newcomer sees first and is told is the main point, not about whether the counter-figure exists somewhere.

---

## 1. "Who pays?" opens with the top 1%'s share of Income Tax, on the home page and the who-pays page

**Where:** The "Who pays?" card on `index.html`, and the first headline figure on `who-pays.html`.

**Exact words:** "**27.2%** of all Income Tax was paid by the top 1% of Income Tax payers in 2023 to 2024." The who-pays body then says: "Higher-income people pay most Income Tax." The first chart is "Top 1% of taxpayers 27.2% / Top 10% 59.1% / Top 50% 90.1% / Bottom 50% of taxpayers 9.9%". The long-run chart's headline is "The top 1%'s share of Income Tax was higher than their share of income in every year from 1999-00 to 2023-24".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** the rich already carry the tax system, and half of taxpayers pay almost nothing. This is the standard opening of the right-of-centre case against higher taxes on top earners.

**A neutral choice:** answer "who pays?" first with all taxes as a share of income. The site has this figure: the lowest-income fifth paid 36.7% of gross income and the highest-income fifth 36.2%. Income Tax is only about 30% of all tax. If the HMRC figure leads, its bar chart should show the income share beside each tax share. At present that pairing is only in the table under "More on…", except for the single 12.4% on the home page.

## 2. Who pays is shown in pounds per household; the share of income goes to another page

**Where:** The headline figures and section 2 of `who-pays.html`.

**Exact words:** "**£51,903** a year in all taxes, on average, by the households of the highest-income fifth" and "**£8,625** … the lowest-income fifth". The section 2 bar chart is in £ a year, from £8,625 to £51,903. The proportional view is one link away: "For how big these taxes are compared with each group's income, see how much of household income goes on tax."

**What a newcomer takes from it:** the top fifth pays about six times as much as the bottom fifth. On a page called "Who pays?", that reads as "the rich pay far more".

**A neutral choice:** show the same ONS Table 2a data as a percentage of gross income next to the cash amounts on this page: 36.7% for the lowest fifth against 36.2% for the highest. That figure says the poorest fifth gives up a slightly larger share. The cash figures say almost the opposite. Only the cash version is the headline here.

## 3. "Who receives more in benefits than they pay in taxes?"

**Where:** Section 4 of `who-pays.html`.

**Exact words:** "In 2023/24, **53.3%** of people lived in households that received more in benefits and services than they paid in taxes." The chart runs from 1977, and the "everyone" line rises from 37.0% to 53.3%.

**What a newcomer takes from it:** most people are now net takers, and the share has been rising for decades. This is the "makers and takers" / "dependency" framing. Putting it under a "Who pays?" heading invites the reader to sort people into payers and receivers.

**A neutral choice:** leave out the binary headcount. Or, if it stays, lead with the limitation that the page itself states: "It does not follow people through their lives… the same person can be on either side at different times". Lead also with the fact that the count includes children's state schooling and the NHS as "benefits". A neutral page could show net position by income fifth and by life stage instead of one majority headcount. The retired/non-retired split (90.1% against 45.8%) softens this, but the 53.3% headline is what newcomers remember.

## 4. The finding that the poorest fifth pays the highest share gets extra caveats; the top fifth's share gets none

**Where:** `household-tax.html`, the opening section and "Why the same tax can look bigger or smaller".

**Exact words:**
- "The share was similar for the lowest-income fifth of people (36.7%) and the highest-income fifth (36.2%), **but for different reasons**."
- "For people in the lowest-income fifth, most is indirect (21.0%), **because their households spend more of their income, some of it from savings or borrowing**."
- A whole section, "Why the same tax can look bigger or smaller", shows that against spending, indirect taxes are about flat (16.5% for the lowest fifth, 15.4% for the highest).
- Limits: "The ONS warns this can make direct taxes look high **for the lowest-income fifths**."
- The panel chart's own headline says "Taxes took the largest share of income for the lowest-income fifth in 2023/24". Then the text above it describes the five fifths as "within five percentage points of each other, with the order changing from year to year".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** the apparent regressivity is a measurement quirk, and VAT is not really harder on poorer households. That is a contested position, and it is the one that rebuts the left's usual "VAT is regressive" argument. The top fifth's 36.2% gets no matching explanation of why it may be over- or under-stated, such as income missed by the survey or Corporation Tax left out.

**A neutral choice:** give each end of the distribution the same number of caveats, or none. Report the share of income plainly, as the ONS headline does. Put the denominator discussion in one general methods note, not right next to the lowest fifth's figure.

## 5. National debt as a bill per household: "£100,569 per household"

**Where:** `taxes.html`, "Borrowing is not the same as debt" and the "For scale" paragraph.

**Exact words:** "Debt is the total still owed from all past years: £2,917bn … equal to 93.3% of a year's GDP, or about **£41,978 per person (£100,569 per household)**." Also: "Borrowing was £1,933 per person, or **£4,630 per household**."

**What a newcomer takes from it:** every household effectively owes £100k. This is the household-budget metaphor ("maxing out the nation's credit card") that has long been used to argue for cutting spending.

**A neutral choice:** give debt as a share of GDP, which the site does, and stop there. Or give the per-household figure only with matching context: who holds the debt, the assets on the other side, and the per-household value of GDP. The site attaches a "these are averages across everyone, not anyone's bill" disclaimer to the tax and spending per-head figures. The debt figure has no such disclaimer.

## 6. Debt interest is a top-three headline, using the larger, gross measure

**Where:** The headline figures on `spending.html`.

**Exact words:** "**£11** of every £100 went on interest on government debt". This is one of three headline figures and sits beside total spending and social protection. Further down: "Interest on government debt here is the interest paid out ('gross payments…'). The OBR and the IFS often quote 'net' debt interest, which subtracts interest the public sector receives, so their figure for the same year is lower." The only "why" box on the first section is "Why did interest on government debt rise in 2022-23?"

**What a newcomer takes from it:** debt servicing is one of the three biggest facts about public spending. Interest is not a COFOG area, yet it is pulled up next to the largest areas. Health (£21) is not a headline figure. The fiscal-alarm message is the first thing on the page.

**A neutral choice:** use the net measure that the OBR and IFS use, which the page admits is lower. Leave debt interest inside "general public services", where the site's own classification puts it. Or make the three headline figures the three largest areas: social protection, health and general public services.

## 7. The receipts-and-spending chart is headlined as spending exceeding receipts

**Where:** `taxes.html`, "Receipts and spending since 1948-49". This is the default "Share of GDP" view.

**Exact words:** Chart headline: "**Spending was higher than receipts in 68 of the 78 years** from 1948-49 to 2025-26". The "after inflation" view has the same form: "Spending was higher than receipts in 65 of the 71 years". The page also lists borrowing as a separate kind of money and says it "adds to the national debt".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** governments chronically overspend, and spending is the problem. The sentence makes spending the subject that went wrong. On a page about taxes, the headline could have described the tax share of GDP instead. That share is now near post-war highs: 40.1% of GDP in 2025-26, with a forecast of 42.9%.

**A neutral choice:** a headline that describes both lines without assigning fault. For example: "Receipts and spending both moved between about 31% and 52% of GDP; the government borrowed in 68 of 78 years." Or headline the chart's subject, receipts.

## 8. Spending growth: a 2003-04 base, "rising in most years", a ten-year window that starts after the 2010-15 cuts, and a truncated axis

**Where:** `spending.html`, "How has spending changed since 2003-04?" and "Change by area over ten years: 2015-16 to 2025-26".

**Exact words:**
- "After adjusting for rising prices, total spending in 2025-26 was **54% higher** than in 2003-04."
- The default after-inflation chart's summary: "Rose from £882bn in 2003-04 to £1,360bn in 2025-26, **rising in most years**." The table shows real spending at £1,116bn in 2010-11 and £1,124bn in 2018-19, roughly flat for eight years with falls in several of them. The summary does not mention this.
- The ten-year bar chart shows every area rising except recreation (−4%): health +34%, defence +29%, "Housing and community amenities 64% rise". The note says "The two years are a fixed ten years apart, not chosen for the result", but 2015-16 falls after most of the 2010-15 reductions.
- None of these figures are per person. The UK population and the number of pensioners grew over the period, so the "11% rise" in social protection is smaller per head.
- The "Share of GDP" chart's y-axis runs from **35 to 55**, not 0. That makes the rise from 38.7% to 44.3% fill much of the chart's height. The receipts-and-spending chart on `taxes.html` starts at 0.

**What a newcomer takes from it:** public spending has grown a lot and steadily, there was no real squeeze, and nearly every service has had more money. This is the right-of-centre answer to "austerity cut public services".

**A neutral choice:** show real spending per person. Describe the 2010-11 to 2019-20 plateau in the chart summary. Use a ten-year window that is not short of the 2009-10 peak, or show both 2009-10 and 2015-16 as bases. Start the share-of-GDP axis at zero, as the taxes page does.

## 9. International comparison: "spending above the average", using a simple OECD average weighted by low-spending countries

**Where:** `international.html`, and the link from the home page.

**Exact words:** "In 2024, UK taxes were 34.4% of GDP, close to the OECD average of 34.1%. Government spending was 46.2% of GDP, **above the average of 44.5%**." Also: "The 'OECD average' is the OECD's simple average of its member countries, each counted equally."

**What a newcomer takes from it:** the UK is a high-spending country whose spending outruns its taxes. The benchmark is pulled down by Mexico (29.2%), Chile (27.5%), Ireland (22.3%, distorted by multinational GDP), Colombia and Costa Rica. Against comparable large European economies, the UK is a low spender: France 57.3%, Germany 49.4%, Italy 50.4%.

**A neutral choice:** give a peer group as well, such as the G7 or high-income European countries. Or say the UK's rank in words in the headline instead of "above the average". The page does say "16 spent a larger share of GDP than the UK", but that is below the headline and the chart.

## 10. "Everyday life" is measured with indicators that mostly read as rising prosperity

**Where:** `index.html` ("Everyday life, year by year") and `life/index.html`.

**Exact words:** The five topics are work, earnings, housing, holidays and class sizes. "**58.4 million** holiday visits abroad by Great Britain residents in 2025", "about 86 for every 100 residents". Employment: "75.3%, higher than in 14 of the other 17 years shown." Earnings: "£767, **higher than in 15 of the other 17 years** shown". The home-page housing card leads with "**65%** of households in England owned their home".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** everyday life is fine or improving. People holiday abroad in large numbers, most own their homes, employment is near its highest, and pay is higher than in most recent years. On the life page, earnings get a rank ("higher than in 15 of 17") instead of the plainer reading: real median pay in 2025 (£767) was still below 2008 (£771), with no growth in 17 years. The page does say "about the same as in April 2008" in the text, but the chart caption leads with the rank. Holidays abroad is an unusual choice of everyday-life indicator, and an affluence one. There is nothing on poverty, real disposable income, food insecurity, NHS waiting times or homelessness. Those indicators are more often cited on the left.

**A neutral choice:** choose topics that include both hardship and affluence measures, such as relative or absolute poverty or real household disposable income alongside holidays. Describe pay against its 2008 level in the chart caption, not by its rank among recent years. Lead the home-page housing card with the private-renting trend that `life/index.html` charts, or show both.

### Also considered, left out as weaker

- `taxes.html` quotes HMRC on frozen thresholds ("Why are more people paying Income Tax?"), and `household-tax.html` quotes the ONS on "fiscal drag". This repeats the "stealth tax" theme twice, but both are the publishers' own explanations.
- `taxes.html` introduces the phrase "tax burden". It is attributed to news reports, so the site does not adopt it.
- `household-tax.html` quotes the ONS on the 12.2× gap before taxes and benefits narrowing to 3.3× after. This stresses how redistributive the system already is. It is a standard ONS headline, though.
- The suggested comparison on `life/index.html` starts in recession-hit 2009. The page says so itself ("Timing matters"), and the comparison shows pay falling, so it does not clearly lean either way.

Count: 10.
