# Impartiality critic: the case that the site leans against the left

Side argued: **left** (that the site's choices nudge a newcomer away from left-leaning conclusions, i.e. towards the right).
Pages read: `site/index.html`, `spending.html`, `spending-areas.html`, `spending-history.html`, `taxes.html`, `who-pays.html`, `who-pays-households.html`, `household-tax.html`, `borrowing.html`, `international.html`, `methods.html`, `life/index.html`, `life/earnings.html`, `life/compare/2011-2019.html`.

Ten examples, strongest first.

---

## 1. The ten-year spending window starts after the deepest austerity cuts

**Page:** `spending.html`, section "Change by area over ten years: 2015-16 to 2025-26".

**Exact words:** "After adjusting for inflation. The two years are a fixed ten years apart, not chosen for the result." Then nine of ten bars are rises: "Health 34% rise", "Defence 29% rise", "Public order and safety (police, courts, prisons, fire) 33% rise", "Education 7% rise", "Housing and community amenities 64% rise". Only "Recreation, culture and religion -4% fall".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** almost every public service has had large real rises, so claims of underfunding or cuts look wrong. Starting in 2015-16 leaves out 2010-11 to 2015-16, when the cuts were deepest. The site's own figures show it: education after inflation peaked at "£137bn in 2010-11, then fell to £118bn in 2019-20" (`spending-history.html`), and the same page says "From 2010-11 to 2019-20 ... spending per person changed by -4.5%". Neither fact gets a bar, a heading or a summary card. Calling the window "not chosen for the result" tells the reader to trust a window that hides the period the left most wants examined.

**Neutral choice:** show the change by area from 2010-11 (the start of a spending regime) as well as the rolling ten years, or let the reader pick the start year. Put the -4.5% per-person fall for 2010-11 to 2019-20 in a summary card, as prominent as "54% higher than in 2003-04".

## 2. The featured comparison of two years is 2011 against 2019, and everything rises

**Page:** `life/index.html` ("Compare two years") and `index.html`. The default button is "Compare 2011 and 2019", described as "the earliest and latest years in which all five topics can be compared".

**Exact words** (`life/compare/2011-2019.html`, "In brief"): "The share of people aged 16 to 64 in work went up from 70.5% ... to 76.1%"; "Median full-time weekly pay, after inflation, went up from £735 ... to £749 ... +1.8%"; "Holiday visits abroad for every 100 residents went up from 68 ... to 88 ... +30%". Top of "Largest changes": "Holidays: Holiday visits abroad, 42.7 million → 58.7 million (+37%)".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** the first comparison the site offers covers exactly the Coalition and Conservative austerity years, and it reads as a run of good news: more jobs, higher pay, many more foreign holidays. The topics are ones that improved, so the period looks benign. Both end years sit at local lows and highs (2011 just after the pay fall, 2019 the employment and holiday peak).

**Neutral choice:** default to the latest year against a fixed earlier year (for example, ten years before), or show no default pair. Include at least one topic that measures hardship, so the default view cannot be all improvement by construction.

## 3. "Everyday life" leaves out poverty, hardship and public-service strain, and includes foreign holidays

**Pages:** `index.html` ("Everyday life, year by year"), `life/index.html`, `methods.html`.

**Exact words:** the five topics are "Work", "Earnings", "Housing", "Holidays", "Class sizes". The homepage card says "Holidays 58.4 million holiday visits abroad by Great Britain residents in 2025", and the life page says "Most visited country abroad: Spain". `methods.html` admits: "Poverty and hardship have official series that the site does not show yet."

**What a newcomer takes from it:** everyday life in the UK means rising employment, home ownership around 65%, steady class sizes and record foreign travel. There is nothing on relative or absolute poverty, child poverty, material deprivation, homelessness, NHS waiting times or real disposable income at the bottom, though all have official series. The site admits these series exist. Leaving them out while including holidays abroad paints a rosier picture of living standards, which helps the case that things are fine and undercuts the case for redistribution.

**Neutral choice:** give poverty and hardship (for example, HBAI relative poverty and material deprivation, which the source list suggests is partly held already) the same footing as holidays. Or drop holidays from the headline set until hardship can be shown beside it.

## 4. "Who pays?" leads with the top 1%'s share of Income Tax

**Pages:** `index.html` card "Who pays? 27.2%" and `who-pays.html` (first headline card and first section).

**Exact words:** "The top 1% of Income Tax payers owed 27.2% of all Income Tax in 2023 to 2024." "Higher-income people pay most Income Tax." Second card: "46.0% of all taxes paid by UK households in 2023/24 were paid by the richest fifth". Chart title: "The top 1%'s share of Income Tax was higher than their share of income in every year".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** the rich carry the tax system, so raising their taxes would be unfair or self-defeating. This is the familiar right-of-centre talking point. The measure that answers "who pays" relative to ability to pay, all taxes as a share of each fifth's income, is almost flat (36.7% lowest fifth, 36.2% highest). On this page it appears only inside a sub-clause ("A share of the total, not the share of income that goes on tax (36.2%, see Tax and income)"). Income Tax is about 28% of household-relevant taxes, yet it frames the whole question. Nothing on the page sets the 1%'s rising share of income or of wealth beside their rising share of tax.

**Neutral choice:** lead "Who pays?" with all taxes as a share of income by fifth (the household-tax figures). Present the Income Tax concentration as one view among others, and put the top 1%'s share of income in the headline sentence with equal weight.

## 5. "53.3% of people received more in benefits than they paid in taxes"

**Page:** `who-pays.html`, section 4, "Who receives more in benefits than they pay in taxes?"

**Exact words:** "In 2023/24, 53.3% of people lived in households that received more in benefits and services than they paid in taxes. That was 90.1% of people in retired households and 45.8% in non-retired households." Chart title: "The share for people in retired households was higher than for people in non-retired households in every year".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** most people are "net takers", so the state is too big. This is the "makers and takers" framing. It counts the NHS and state schools as "benefits" received, which makes almost any family with children or any ill person a net recipient. A one-year snapshot is offered as the fourth of five answers to "Who pays?". A "Limits" note says it is one year at a time, but the headline sentence and the section title carry the message.

**Neutral choice:** don't frame shared public services as "benefits received" in a "who pays" page. If shown, lead with the ONS's own redistribution finding (quoted on `household-tax.html`: the richest-to-poorest income ratio falls "from 12.2 times ... to 3.3 times"). Or present lifetime or life-cycle context in the headline, not only in Limits.

## 6. Caveats are attached only to the finding that the poorest pay the highest share

**Pages:** `index.html` ("How much household income goes on tax?"), `who-pays.html` section 2 Limits, `household-tax.html`.

**Exact words:** homepage: "The lowest-income fifth paid 36.7% of its gross income in tax, and the highest-income fifth paid 36.2%" is followed at once by "the lowest-income fifth paid 16.5% of its spending in indirect taxes, and the other fifths between 15.4% and 17.4%. ONS warns that lump sums of tax put off from earlier years can make the lowest fifths' direct taxes look high." `household-tax.html` adds a box, "Why the same tax can look bigger or smaller", and a table that measures the poorest fifth's VAT against spending.

**What a newcomer takes from it:** the finding that the poorest fifth pays the largest share of income in tax, mainly through VAT, is a statistical artefact. VAT is about the same for everyone. No similar warning sits beside the right-friendly figures: the top-1% share, the 46% share and the 53.3% net recipients. The homepage picks the measure that makes VAT look flat (share of spending: "close to the other fifths"). It leaves out the measure that shows it as clearly regressive (share of disposable income: 24.9% for the lowest fifth against 10.5% for the highest). That figure appears only in a table further down `household-tax.html`.

**Neutral choice:** either put caveats of equal weight on every headline or on none. Where a card gives indirect tax against spending, give it against disposable income in the same place: 24.9% against 10.5%.

## 7. Real pay is never compared with 2008, so the "lost decade" of pay never appears

**Pages:** `index.html` ("Earnings after inflation"), `life/index.html`, `life/earnings.html`.

**Exact words:** "After inflation, pay in April 2025 was about the same as in April 2021." "The dark dot is 2025: £767, the highest of the 5 years measured the same way." Earnings page: "This page therefore gives no single change from 2008 to 2014."

**What a newcomer takes from it:** pay is flat to slightly up, at a high. The site's own table shows the median at £771 in April 2008 against £767 in April 2025 (2025 prices). Seventeen years with no real rise is the biggest living-standards story in the data, and a central left argument. The site keeps job-coding breaks so it never states this, and the label "highest of the 5 years" cuts the 2008 peak out of the comparison. Overlapping data across the 2011 coding change exists and is widely used; ONS publishes both bases for 2011. The site still picks the 2021 start, which shows no change.

**Neutral choice:** state, with the caveat, that median real pay in 2025 was roughly at its 2008 level. Or use the ONS's own long-run real earnings measure (for example, AWE) for the long view, and don't describe 2025 as a high.

## 8. National debt is shown per person and per household, and deficits get a running count

**Page:** `taxes.html`, "Borrowing is not the same as debt" and "Receipts and spending since 1948-49".

**Exact words:** "£2,917bn ... or about £41,978 per person (£100,569 per household)". "Borrowing was £1,933 per person, or £4,630 per household." Chart title: "Spending was higher than receipts in 68 of the 78 years from 1948-49 to 2025-26". Also the headline card: "£134bn borrowed to cover spending above receipts".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** each household is "on the hook" for £100,000. Governments chronically overspend. Borrowing is a household-budget problem. Per-household debt is the standard framing of fiscal-restraint campaigns. The disclaimer ("not anyone's bill") follows the number it disclaims, after the reader has already taken it in. Public sector assets are never shown per household, nor what borrowing pays for (investment).

**Neutral choice:** give debt as a share of GDP only (the measure the OBR and IMF use for comparison), or pair any per-household debt figure with public sector assets or net worth per household. Title the long chart by what it shows ("Receipts and spending as a share of GDP since 1948-49") rather than counting deficit years.

## 9. Debt interest is shown as the larger, gross figure, repeated across the site

**Pages:** `index.html`, `spending.html`.

**Exact words:** "General public services take £13, of which £11 is interest paid on debt, counted before interest received. The OBR and IFS often quote a lower figure that subtracts interest received." On `spending.html` the same "£11 of every £100" appears in the table note, the explanatory paragraph and the "Limits". An ONS quote box asks "Why did interest on government debt rise in 2022-23?".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** debt interest is a huge and growing drain, about one pound in nine, so borrowing to invest is reckless. The site admits the official fiscal watchdogs use the lower net figure. It still leads with the gross one, and repeats it three or four times on one page, more than any service area except the top two.

**Neutral choice:** use the OBR/IFS net debt interest figure as the headline, the measure fiscal commentary uses, with gross in a note. Or show both side by side once.

## 10. The debt page's second headline is that UK borrowing costs are the highest in the G7

**Page:** `borrowing.html`, opening paragraph and section "How much does each government pay to borrow?"

**Exact words:** "the market rate (yield) for new 10-year UK government borrowing averaged 4.58% a year, the highest of the G7. The latest month, September 2026, was 5.24%, the highest of the G7." Repeated in the section body: "the highest of the G7: higher than the United States, Italy, France, Canada, Germany and Japan."

**What a newcomer takes from it:** markets are punishing the UK, so it must cut borrowing ("bond vigilantes"). This framing argues against left-leaning fiscal expansion. Gilt yields partly reflect higher UK inflation and Bank Rate rather than fiscal risk. The page quotes the DMO and OBR on this further down, but the opening sentence states the ranking twice with no context. A single latest month (September 2026) is singled out on top of the annual average.

**Neutral choice:** give the annual average only, beside each country's inflation or policy rate. Or open with the debt comparison alone (UK second lowest in the G7) and keep yields in their section with the explanation before the ranking.

### Notes on balance (for the judge)

Some features cut the other way, and I have not counted them. The `international.html` page shows UK tax near the OECD average. The per-person spending caveat ("the total overstates how much spending per person has grown") appears. ONS's redistribution quote appears on `household-tax.html`. The earnings page does disclose the 2011-2014 fall in its notes. My argument is about what each page leads with and repeats, not about what is hidden in tables.
