# Impartiality critic: the case that the site leans left (is biased against the right)

Site read: built `site/` at d72127d (index, spending, taxes, who-pays, household-tax, life/ pages incl. year pages, methods, glossary, international, borrowing, who-pays-households, who-pays-age).

Ten examples, strongest first. Honest caveat up front: the site is careful, and much of it is neutral or even right-friendly (top 1% Income Tax share as a homepage headline, "richest fifth paid 46.0%", spending up 54% after inflation since 2003-04, employment near record highs). The case below is about where the editorial choices — the sub-periods singled out, the quotes picked to "explain" changes, the caveats attached to some figures and not others — tilt against the right.

---

## 1. Spending: the only sub-period singled out is the Conservative/Coalition decade, framed as a per-person cut

**Page:** `spending.html`, "How has spending changed since 2003-04?"

**Exact words:** "Per person, after inflation, it was 32% higher than in 2003-04. The population grew 17% over the same years, so the total overstates how much spending per person has grown. From 2010-11 to 2019-20 total spending after inflation changed by 1.4%, but spending per person changed by -4.5%."

**What a newcomer takes from it:** spending in general has not really grown as much as it looks, and the one era worth calling out is 2010–2020 (Coalition and Conservative governments), when spending per person *fell*. That is the "austerity" story in all but name.

**Why it is a choice, not a given:** the methods page promises "fixed windows (ten years for spending by area, the first and latest year for each series)... so years are not picked for the result". 2010-11 to 2019-20 is neither of those; it is exactly the span of one party's time in office. The same table shows per-person spending (2025-26 prices) rose from £14,790 (2003-04) to £17,679 (2009-10), about +20% under Labour, and from £16,990 (2019-20) to £19,574 (2025-26), about +15% — neither is highlighted. The sentence "the total overstates how much spending per person has grown" is also editorial: it argues against the "spending has ballooned" reading.

**Neutral choice:** give the first-to-latest figure only, or give per-person change for every comparable sub-period (e.g. each decade, or each change of government) on the same footing.

## 2. Year pages 2011–2017 carry a quote blaming a named government policy for falling real pay; Labour-era pages get a data-quality caveat instead

**Pages:** `life/2011.html` to `life/2017.html` and `life/earnings.html`, box "What did the ONS say about pay in the decade after the 2008 recession?"

**Exact words (ONS quote):** "Earnings have lagged behind prices for most of the decade since the start of the recession. In the public sector, a pay freeze (from 2011) and pay cap (from 2013) kept wage rises below inflation..."

**What a newcomer takes from it:** seven consecutive year pages tell the reader that Coalition/Conservative policy held down wages. It is the only explanatory quote on the everyday-life pages that names a specific government policy as the cause of a household hardship.

**The asymmetry:** the site's banner on every page is "Change is not cause", yet this causal quote is repeated on every year of that government. The 2023–2025 pages (2024–25 being the current Labour government's first full period) get "How reliable are the latest employment figures?", a quote about Labour Force Survey response rates — which, if anything, invites doubt about weak recent labour-market data rather than linking it to any policy (e.g. the April 2025 employer National Insurance rise, which official bodies have discussed). Explanatory quotes for 2008–2010 talk about "the recession", not policy.

**Neutral choice:** either no policy-attributing quotes on year pages, or an equivalent official explanation for every period, including recent changes under the current government.

## 3. "Tax burden" is put in scare quotes, disowned, and pre-rebutted; the site never says how high today's tax take is historically

**Pages:** `taxes.html` ("Taxes alone were 36.0% of GDP in 2025-26, a share news reports often call the 'tax burden'"); `glossary.html`, entry "'Tax burden'": "This site does not use the name itself... It is not what any one person or household pays: when this share goes up, it does not mean everyone pays more".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** "tax burden" is a loaded media phrase, and worries about it are a misunderstanding. The OBR — one of the site's own core sources — uses the term routinely; it is not a partisan coinage.

**Compounding choice:** the receipts chart starts at 1948-49, so its "in words" summary says receipts' "highest 43.3% (1949-50)" and the reader is never told that the tax share in 2025-26, and the OBR forecast rising to 2031-32, is at or near the highest since the post-war years. The site readily says when something is "the highest since" in other contexts (OBR quote on gilt yields "higher... than at any point since the start of the century"; ONS on borrowing "the highest such ratio since the end of World War Two").

**Neutral choice:** use the OBR's term plainly, as the site does with other official terms, and state where 2025-26 and the forecast stand against the post-war record, as the site does for borrowing.

## 4. The homepage's "how much income goes on tax" panel leads with the poorest paying as much as the richest

**Page:** `index.html` (and `household-tax.html` lead).

**Exact words:** "The lowest-income fifth paid 36.7% of its gross income in tax, and the highest-income fifth paid 36.2%. For the lowest, most of it is indirect tax such as VAT (21.0% of their gross income)."

**What a newcomer takes from it:** the UK tax system is flat or regressive; the poor pay as big a share as the rich. This is a long-standing left talking point.

**Why it is contestable:** it divides by *gross income*, while the bottom fifth spends more than its income (the site's own table: indirect tax is 21.0% of gross income but 16.5% of spending), and the bottom fifth includes retirees and students drawing on savings or loans. ONS itself warns that deferred lump-sum tax payments make the lowest fifths' direct taxes "look high". These points appear, but as smaller caveats after the headline. The household-tax page then adds a boxed question "Why can indirect taxes be a large share of income for lower-income households?", reinforcing the frame.

**Neutral choice:** lead with a measure that does not depend on the contested denominator — e.g. share of all household taxes paid (bottom fifth 7.7%, top fifth 46.0%) or indirect tax as a share of spending (near flat, 15.4%–17.4%) — and give the gross-income figure as one of several.

## 5. The one right-friendly homepage headline comes with an attached rebuttal; the left-friendly one does not

**Page:** `index.html`, "Who pays?" panel.

**Exact words:** "The top 1% of Income Tax payers owed 27.2% of all Income Tax in 2023 to 2024. They had 12.4% of all Income Tax payers' income. Every household also pays taxes on spending, such as VAT."

**What a newcomer takes from it:** "yes, the rich pay a lot of Income Tax, but don't forget everyone pays VAT" — the final sentence exists to qualify the point.

**The asymmetry:** the panel beside it ("lowest-income fifth paid 36.7%") gets no comparable counterweight in the headline (e.g. "most of the lowest fifth's gross income is cash benefits" or "the highest fifth paid 46.0% of all household taxes"). On `who-pays.html`, the top-1% chart is followed by an HMRC quote about "forestalling" and the share then *falling* because of threshold freezes, while the second headline stat is again the lowest fifth's £8,625 / 36.7%.

**Neutral choice:** state both headline figures without a "but" sentence, or attach equally weighted context to each.

## 6. Debt page: a question chosen to explain away the UK's G7-highest borrowing costs, under a reassuring debt headline

**Page:** `borrowing.html`.

**Exact words:** headline "UK government debt was 101.3% of GDP, the second lowest of the seven G7 countries"; then a box titled "Have borrowing costs risen only in the UK?" quoting the OBR: "Government borrowing costs have risen across the world..."

**What a newcomer takes from it:** UK debt is comparatively modest, and the UK's highest-in-G7 gilt yields (4.58% in 2025, 5.24% in September 2026 — under the current government) are a global phenomenon, not a judgement on UK fiscal policy.

**Why it is a choice:** the question framing ("risen only in the UK?") presupposes the answer and serves as a defence of the incumbent government's record. No official text on UK-specific factors (e.g. market reaction to UK fiscal events) is quoted, though the DMO and OBR have both published such commentary. Leading with debt rank (where the UK looks good) rather than yields or net interest (where it is highest or third highest) also softens the fiscal picture that fiscal conservatives emphasise.

**Neutral choice:** a neutrally worded heading ("What do official bodies say about UK borrowing costs?") and lead with the measures in a fixed order regardless of how the UK ranks.

## 7. International comparison benchmarks UK tax against an unweighted OECD average that includes Mexico, Colombia, Chile and Turkey

**Page:** `international.html` (also linked from the homepage).

**Exact words:** "In 2024, UK taxes were 34.4% of GDP, close to the OECD average of 34.1%"; "UK taxes were the second lowest as a share of GDP among the G7".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** UK taxes are ordinary or low; complaints about high UK taxes are overblown.

**Why it is a choice:** the "OECD average" counts each country equally, including much poorer middle-income economies with low tax takes; the site admits "Iceland counts as much as the United States" only in the Limits. The page does not show the UK's own recent rise (from 29.1% in 1996 to 35.2% in 2022) in its lead, nor compare with the UK's own history, which is the comparison voters actually feel. Spending, by contrast, is described plainly as "above the average".

**Neutral choice:** lead with the UK's own trend plus a like-for-like peer group (e.g. high-income OECD countries, or a GDP-weighted average), with the simple average as one line.

## 8. Debt per household is shown and then immediately disowned as "not anyone's bill"

**Page:** `taxes.html`, "Borrowing is not the same as debt".

**Exact words:** "...about £41,978 per person (£100,569 per household). That is the debt divided by everyone, an average, not anyone's bill: it is owed by the public sector, not by people or households."

**What a newcomer takes from it:** public debt is not really the public's problem. That downplays the conservative argument that debt is ultimately serviced and repaid from future taxes (debt interest is already £11 of every £100 spent on services, per the spending page).

**Related framing:** the receipts chart title "Spending was higher than receipts in 68 of the 78 years from 1948-49 to 2025-26" presents deficits as the normal state of affairs.

**Neutral choice:** say the debt is owed by the public sector and serviced from public revenue, i.e. ultimately by taxpayers, without the "not anyone's bill" line; title the chart descriptively ("Receipts and spending as a share of GDP since 1948-49").

## 9. Choice of everyday topics and quotes favours left-coded concerns; right-coded official series are absent

**Pages:** `index.html` ("Quick answers: Women's employment, Pay after inflation, Class sizes, Holidays, Renting"); `methods.html` ("Poverty and hardship have official series that the site does not show yet."); `life/housing.html` quotes.

**What a newcomer takes from it:** the "everyday life" story is about stagnant pay, renting and affordability. The housing quotes selected are "71% of private renters in the lowest two income quintiles spent more than 30% of their gross household income on rent" and first-time buyers needing 30-year-plus mortgages. The methods page signals poverty and hardship as the next topics.

**What is missing:** official multi-year series that right-leaning readers would regard as everyday concerns — crime (ONS Crime Survey), net migration (ONS), economic inactivity and the number claiming sickness/disability benefits (DWP/ONS), NHS waiting lists against health spending, public-sector productivity. None is shown or flagged as "not shown yet".

**Neutral choice:** a topic list chosen by a stated, side-blind rule, and if future topics are named, name a balanced set (e.g. crime and benefit caseloads alongside poverty).

## 10. Social-protection spending is framed around pensions, not working-age welfare

**Page:** `spending.html` and `index.html`.

**Exact words:** "Social protection (pensions, benefits, social care)"; "mainly State Pension, other benefits and social care"; "Pensions in old age cost £155bn in 2025-26, inside social protection."

**What a newcomer takes from it:** the biggest spending area is mostly pensions — a sympathetic, hard-to-cut category. The one sub-item the site pulls out of social protection is pensions; working-age benefits (incapacity, Universal Credit, housing benefit), whose growth is a central right-of-centre concern, are never separated out even though HM Treasury's PESA social protection lines (the site's own source) split them by function (e.g. sickness and disability, family, unemployment, housing).

**Neutral choice:** show the PESA sub-functions of social protection side by side, so pensions and working-age benefits are equally visible.

---

**Count: 10 examples.** The strongest are 1–3 (singled-out austerity window that breaks the site's own fixed-window rule; policy-blaming quote repeated only on Conservative-era year pages; "tax burden" disowned with no historical-high context). Examples 7–10 are weaker, being matters of emphasis and omission rather than wording.
