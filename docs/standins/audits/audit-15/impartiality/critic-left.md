# Impartiality critic: LEFT

Side argued: that the built site leans to the **left** in UK politics, meaning its choices would nudge a newcomer towards left-leaning conclusions.

Pages read: `site/index.html`, `spending.html`, `spending-areas.html`, `spending-history.html`, `taxes.html`, `tax-history.html`, `who-pays.html`, `who-pays-households.html`, `who-pays-age.html`, `household-tax.html`, `international.html`, `borrowing.html`, `glossary.html`, `methods.html`, `life/index.html`, `life/earnings.html`, `life/housing.html`, `life/work.html`, `life/schools.html` and `life/compare/2011-2019.html`.

Overall: the site is careful. It gives sources, breaks series where definitions change and quotes official bodies instead of explaining things itself. Several things it does point the other way, for example leading "Who pays?" with the top 1%'s share of Income Tax, using gross rather than net debt interest, and saying "Poverty and hardship are not measured here". The examples below are the strongest honest case for a left lean that I could make from what is on the pages. Most of them come from framing and emphasis, not from wrong numbers. I found **nine** that I think hold up. A tenth would have been padding, so I stopped at nine.

---

## 1. Home page: the "poorest pay about the same share as the richest" headline, without the caveats

**Page:** `index.html`, card "How much household income goes on tax?". The same pairing is in the hero of `who-pays.html` and the lead of `household-tax.html`.

**Exact words:** "The lowest-income fifth paid 36.7% of its gross income in tax, and the highest-income fifth paid 36.2%. For the lowest, most of it is indirect tax such as VAT (21.0% of their gross income)." The bar list beneath gives 36.7 / 30.7 / 30.2 / 31.7 / 36.2. The hero of `who-pays.html` sets "£8,625 … (36.7% of their gross income)" beside "£51,903 … (36.2% of their gross income)".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** the UK tax system is not progressive overall, and the poorest pay a higher share than the richest. This is a familiar campaigning line from the left (Equality Trust, TUC).

**What is missing at the point of the headline:** the caveats that weaken it are on deeper pages only.
- **Deferred tax:** ONS warns that lump-sum deferred tax payments "can make direct taxes look high for the lowest-income fifths". The word "deferred" is in `household-tax.html` (Limits) and its sense is in `who-pays-households.html`, but not on `index.html` or `who-pays.html`.
- **Indirect tax measured against spending:** the bottom fifth pays 16.5% of spending, about the same as every other fifth (15.4–17.4%). This is the site's own second table, two clicks down.
- **Overall effect of taxes and benefits:** ONS's own headline is that taxes and benefits cut the richest-to-poorest income ratio from 12.2 times to 3.3 times. That is quoted only in a collapsed section of `household-tax.html`.

**Neutral choice:** where the 36.7% vs 36.2% pair appears on the home page, add one line saying that against spending, indirect tax is about 16–17% for every fifth, and that ONS warns deferred tax lump sums inflate the lowest fifths' direct tax. Alternatively, lead with the direct-tax gradient (15.7% to 28.8%) and put the all-tax ratio second.

## 2. Taxes page: "Spending was higher than receipts in 68 of the 78 years" presented as a chart headline

**Page:** `taxes.html`, "Receipts and spending since 1948-49".

**Exact words:** chart title "Spending was higher than receipts in 68 of the 78 years from 1948-49 to 2025-26". The after-inflation view says "… in 65 of the 71 years".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** deficits are the normal state of affairs, so today's borrowing is nothing unusual. This supports the left-of-centre case against fiscal restraint.

**Why it is a choice:** the site writes a one-line verdict above every chart, and of everything this chart could say, it picked a count that makes deficits look routine. The same chart could have been headed with something equally true, such as debt at 93% of GDP, close to its highest since the series began in 1974-75, or the size of the 2025-26 deficit next to past years. The receipts line is also described without noting that the forecast 42.9% of GDP for 2031-32 would be the highest since 1949-50 (see point 4).

**Neutral choice:** a descriptive title that states no verdict ("Receipts and spending as a share of GDP, 1948-49 to 2031-32"), or a headline about the latest year's gap.

## 3. Avoiding the official term "tax burden", with extra warnings that apply only to it

**Pages:** `taxes.html` and the `glossary.html` entry "“Tax burden”".

**Exact words:** on the taxes page, "Taxes alone were 36.0% of GDP in 2025-26, a share news reports often call the “tax burden”. It compares all tax … so it is not anyone's tax bill". In the glossary: "This site does not use the name itself … when this share goes up, it does not mean everyone pays more, and when it goes down, it does not mean everyone pays less. The Office for Budget Responsibility publishes its own version …"

**What a newcomer takes from it:** "tax burden" is a loaded term from the press, and claims that the tax burden is at a post-war high should be treated with suspicion. In current UK debate that claim is mainly made by the right.

**Why it leans:** the glossary itself says the OBR, the official forecaster, uses the measure, yet the site puts it in scare quotes and attributes it to "news reports". It also gets an unusually long disclaimer ("it does not mean everyone pays more"). Similar figures that cut the other way get no such warning, such as "UK taxes were 34.4% of GDP, close to the OECD average" or "the second lowest tax as a share of GDP" in the G7 (`international.html`).

**Neutral choice:** use the OBR's term in the OBR's own words ("the OBR calls this the tax burden"), and give every %-of-GDP comparison either the same caveat or none.

## 4. The long receipts chart starts at the post-war high, and the forecast high goes unremarked

**Page:** `taxes.html`, "Receipts and spending as a share of GDP".

**Exact words:** "Receipts: from 42.9% in 1948-49, fell to 33.5% in 1960-61, … then rose to 40.1% in 2025-26. In between: highest 43.3% (1949-50). Forecast for 2031-32: 42.9%."

**What a newcomer takes from it:** today's tax take is well within the historical range, because the chart opens at the post-war peak. The fact that the OBR's forecast would take receipts to their highest share of GDP since 1949-50 has to be worked out from the table.

**Why it leans:** the start year is defensible (it is where the databank starts). Even so, the words read the forecast as just another number. Elsewhere the site uses phrases like "the highest of the G7" (bond yields), "higher than in 51 of the other 54 years" (employment) and "the highest such ratio since … 1946" (borrowing, in a quote), so it is willing to give historical rank when it wants to.

**Neutral choice:** add "the forecast for 2031-32 would be the highest share since 1949-50". The data already supports that statement.

## 5. Debt page leads with "second lowest of the G7"

**Page:** `borrowing.html`, first sentence. Linked from `index.html` ("How do UK debt and borrowing costs compare…").

**Exact words:** "In 2024, UK government debt was 101.3% of GDP, the second lowest of the seven G7 countries: lower than Japan, Italy, the United States, France and Canada and higher than Germany."

**What a newcomer takes from it:** UK debt is comparatively modest, so there is room to borrow more. This is a common left-of-centre argument.

**Why it leans:** the G7 is a group of unusually high-debt economies. Measured against the wider OECD, which the site uses as its main comparator on `international.html`, UK debt is well above average (92.5% against 77.1%). That fact comes only in the third paragraph, under a toggle. On the tax page, by contrast, the lead comparison is the OECD average. Choosing the comparator group page by page lets each lead read in the more reassuring direction.

**Neutral choice:** lead both pages with the same comparator, or give the G7 rank and the OECD comparison together in the opening sentence.

## 6. "Owed by the public sector, not by people or households"

**Page:** `taxes.html`, "Borrowing is not the same as debt".

**Exact words:** "… or about £41,978 per person (£100,569 per household). That is the debt divided by everyone, an average, not anyone's bill: it is owed by the public sector, not by people or households."

**What a newcomer takes from it:** national debt is not really a household's concern. This answers the "every family owes £100,000" line of the debt hawks, which is mainly heard on the right.

**Why it leans:** the per-head figure is fine. The added sentence goes beyond describing the figure and rebuts how it is often used. The debt is ultimately serviced from taxes paid by people and households, which the site itself shows elsewhere. The site gives no similarly firm rebuttal to the uses the left makes of figures. For example, the 36.7% vs 36.2% pair (point 1) is not followed by "this is not anyone's tax rate, and the lowest fifth's figure is inflated by …" on the home page.

**Neutral choice:** "This is an average for scale. The debt is owed by the public sector and serviced from public revenue." Leave out "not by people or households".

## 7. Housing: private renting chosen as the lead change, with a hardship quote

**Pages:** `life/index.html` and `life/housing.html` (intro and quoted official words), plus every `life/compare/*.html` page.

**Exact words:**
- Intro: "In 2024-25, 65% of households in England owned their home and 19% rented privately. … In 2008-09, 14% rented privately." The only change over time in the intro is the rise in private renting.
- Compare pages, lead housing figure on every page: "The share of households in England renting privately went up from …"
- Quoted: "On average, 71% of private renters in the lowest two income quintiles spent more than 30% of their gross household income on rent …"
- "House prices compared with earnings": "7.55 times a year's earnings, compared with 3.89 times in 1999".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** a "Generation Rent" story of growing renting and unaffordable housing, which the left uses to argue for regulating rents and building social housing.

**Why it leans:** each choice is defensible on its own. Together they all push in one direction:
- The fixed lead figure could just as well have been the home-ownership rate, which the home page uses. Ownership has been roughly flat at about 63–65% since 2013-14, and private renting has stayed at "around 19% or 20% since 2013-14", in MHCLG's own words quoted on the same page.
- Of four official quotes, the one on cost is a hardship statistic. No quote on, for example, renters' satisfaction or the share of income spent on mortgages over time is offered.

**Neutral choice:** make ownership the lead tenure figure, as on the home page, and put the "steady since 2013-14" point next to the 2008-09 comparison in the intro.

## 8. Pay "about the same" as in 2021, on the home page

**Pages:** `index.html` ("Earnings after inflation"), `life/index.html` and `life/earnings.html`.

**Exact words:** "After inflation, pay in April 2025 was about the same as in April 2021." The "In brief" stretches on the earnings page are "+1.0%" (2011–2020) and "+1.1%" (2006–2010).

**What a newcomer takes from it:** real wages have stagnated, with a lost decade-and-a-half of pay. This is a central left-of-centre theme.

**Why it leans:**
- **Base year:** 2021 is a pandemic year, when lower-paid workers on furlough or out of work were under-represented among full-time employees, so the middle wage was pushed up. Starting from that base understates the recovery.
- **Measure:** the series is full-time median gross pay only.
- **Higher figures elsewhere:** in its own details the site shows pay at the bottom-tenth line up 7% after inflation since 2021, and the 2023 low to 2025 rise (£737 to £767, about +4%). Neither reaches the home page.

The 2021 base is justified by the change in job coding (SOC 2020). Still, picking the headline "about the same" instead of "the lowest-paid tenth saw the largest rise" is a choice of emphasis.

**Neutral choice:** say on the home page that April 2021 was affected by the pandemic, or show the bottom-tenth and median changes together.

## 9. Real spending described as flat for the "austerity decade", in total and without population

**Page:** `spending.html`, "How has spending changed since 2003-04?" (after-inflation view).

**Exact words:** "Rose from £882bn in 2003-04 to £1,360bn in 2025-26, with little change from 2010-11 (£1,116bn) to 2019-20 (£1,132bn)." The share-of-GDP description "fell to a low of 39.1% in 2018-19, stayed at 39.1% until 2019-20".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** spending was held flat from 2010 to 2019, which is the austerity story told by the left. The only stretch singled out by name falls exactly on the Coalition and Conservative years.

**Why it leans (mildly):** the automatic summary picks out flat stretches, and this one is real. But the page offers no per-person view, so it cannot show that spending per head fell over that decade while the population grew, or that it rose sharply before it. The narrower decade is therefore emphasised mainly by the wording. The 10-year by-area window (2015-16 to 2025-26) shows rises everywhere except recreation, which cuts the other way. That is why this point is ranked last.

**Neutral choice:** describe the whole series evenly (for example, "rose from 2003-04 to 2010-11, changed little to 2019-20, then rose") without naming one stretch, or add a per-person view so that each period is measured the same way.
