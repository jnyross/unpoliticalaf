# Impartiality critic: the case that the site leans RIGHT

Scope: built pages in `site/` only (index, spending, spending-areas, spending-history, taxes, tax-history, who-pays, who-pays-age, who-pays-households, household-tax, borrowing, international, methods, life/index, life/work, life/earnings, life/housing, life/2024, life/compare/2011-2019), plus the site's own figure record for UK population (`figures/ons-ukpop.html`). Site version 4cb1bcc.

The site is careful and caveats heavily, so none of these is a smoking gun. Taken together, though, the choices of headline, ordering and framing keep landing on the right's preferred stories: the rich already carry the tax system, most people take out more than they put in, the state has grown fast and austerity barely happened, debt and borrowing are dangerous, and the 2010s were a decade of steady gains. The left's preferred measures (poverty, inequality, wealth, the post-2008 fall in pay) are missing or not worked out.

Ten examples, strongest first.

---

## 1. "Who pays?" leads with the top 1%'s share of Income Tax, on the home page and the who-pays page

**Where / exact words.**
- Home, "Who pays?" card: the big number is **"27.2%"**, with "The top 1% of Income Tax payers owed 27.2% of all Income Tax in 2023 to 2024."
- `who-pays.html`, first stat: "27.2% of all Income Tax for the year was owed by the top 1% of Income Tax payers". First sentence of the page: **"Higher-income people pay most Income Tax."**
- Chart title: "The top 1%'s share of Income Tax was higher than their share of income in every year from 1999-00 to 2023-24."

**What a newcomer takes from it.** The first answer to "who pays?" is that a tiny group of top earners carries the load. This is the standard right-of-centre talking point against higher taxes on the rich. The first sentence states it as a general truth. The chart title adds that this has been so every year.

**Neutral choice.** Income Tax raised about £277bn of £976bn in 2023-24, as the page's own Limits note says. The headline number should cover all taxes, using the ONS household figures the site already has. Those show the lowest and highest fifths paying similar shares of income (36.7% and 36.2%). Failing that, give the 12.4% income share the same visual weight as the 27.2% and title the chart neutrally, for example "Top 1% shares of Income Tax and of income, 1999-00 to 2023-24". The chart could also note that the top 1%'s share of income itself rose, from 11.0% to 12.4%. Drop "Higher-income people pay most Income Tax" as the opening line, or follow it at once with the all-taxes finding.

## 2. A whole section framed as "Who receives more in benefits than they pay in taxes?"

**Where / exact words.** `who-pays.html` section 4 and its chart: "In 2023/24, 53.3% of people lived in households that received more in benefits and services than they paid in taxes. That was 90.1% of people in retired households…". The section is listed in the page menu, and `who-pays-households.html` repeats it with the ONS "net recipients" quote.

**What a newcomer takes from it.** "Most people take out more than they put in." This is the "net contributors versus net recipients" (or "makers and takers") frame that the right uses to argue that welfare and the state are too large. The figure counts the value of state schooling and the NHS as "benefits", and leaves out Corporation Tax and other taxes not allocated to households. Both choices push the share up. The Limits note says so, but the headline and the long chart from 1977 come first.

**Neutral choice.** Present the same ONS table the way the ONS leads with it: what taxes and benefits do to the gap between incomes. The site has this quote on household-tax ("12.2 times … reduced to 3.3 times") but places it lower down on a different page. Alternatively, title the section neutrally ("Taxes paid and benefits received, by household") and put the lifetime caveat in the headline sentence, not in Limits.

## 3. The left's measures are excluded by policy, while the right's are featured

**Where / exact words.** `methods.html`, Principles: **"Poverty and hardship are not measured here."** No page shows income inequality (Gini, 90/10 household income), wealth or its distribution, child poverty, food insecurity, or real benefit levels. The only measure of inequality across the population is the top-1% Income Tax share in example 1. The earnings page has a full-time pay 90/10 ratio, but it covers only full-time employees.

**What a newcomer takes from it.** A site that presents itself as a complete picture of "who pays" and "everyday life" leaves out the distributional outcomes that left-of-centre arguments rest on. It keeps the distributional statistic that right-of-centre arguments rest on. Leaving something out is itself a choice. The 53.3% "net recipient" figure appears, while the share of people in relative poverty, from the same family of official household surveys (HBAI/FRS), does not.

**Neutral choice.** Either include the official HBAI measures of low income (relative and absolute, before and after housing costs) and the ONS Gini alongside the top-1% tax share, or remove both sides' distributional headline statistics. Do not keep only one side's.

## 4. Spending growth is presented as large, and the 2010s squeeze as "little change"

**Where / exact words.** `spending.html`: "After adjusting for rising prices, total spending in 2025-26 was **54% higher** than in 2003-04." The chart in words: "Rose from £882bn in 2003-04 to £1,360bn in 2025-26, **with little change from 2010-11 (£1,116bn) to 2019-20 (£1,132bn)**." The ten-year bar chart (2015-16 to 2025-26) shows nine of ten areas rising: "General public services … 56% rise", "Housing … 64% rise", "Health 34% rise".

**What a newcomer takes from it.** Public spending has ballooned, and there was no real squeeze in the 2010s. Neither figure allows for population, which the site has: 59.6m in mid-2003, 62.8m in 2010, 66.6m in 2019 and 69.5m in 2025, per `figures/ons-ukpop.html`. Per person, the real rise since 2003-04 is about 32%, not 54%. From 2010-11 to 2019-20, real spending per person fell by about 4.5%. "Little change" also hides the fall as a share of GDP over the same years, from 45.5% to 39.1%. The share-of-GDP tab does report that fall, but only on a tab the reader has to switch to. The ten-year window starts in 2015-16, close to the low point, so every bar looks like growth.

**Neutral choice.** Add spending per person after inflation as a view, or at least as the lead sentence. Describe 2010-11 to 2019-20 by what the series did ("flat in total, falling per person and as a share of GDP"). In the area comparison, show a second fixed window (for example 2009-10 to 2019-20), or real per-person change, so that a single base year does not set the impression.

## 5. Debt and borrowing are restated as "per household" bills, and deficits are framed as a habit

**Where / exact words.** `taxes.html`: "Borrowing was £1,933 per person, or **£4,630 per household**." Also: "Debt … £2,917bn … or about £41,978 per person (**£100,569 per household**)". Chart title: "**Spending was higher than receipts in 68 of the 78 years** from 1948-49 to 2025-26" (and "65 of the 71 years" for the other view).

**What a newcomer takes from it.** A six-figure debt "per household" invites the household-budget analogy for public debt, which right-leaning fiscal arguments rely on. The site adds "not anyone's bill", but the £100,569 figure is the vivid part. The chart title turns 78 years of data into a single message: governments nearly always overspend.

**Neutral choice.** Give debt as a share of GDP (93.3%, which is already there) and drop the per-household conversion. Title the chart descriptively ("Receipts and spending as a share of GDP, 1948-49 to 2025-26"), as most other charts on the site are titled.

## 6. The cost of debt is shown at its largest, and the borrowing page leads with market danger

**Where / exact words.**
- Home: "General public services take £13, of which **£11 is interest paid on debt**, counted before interest received." On `spending.html` the area label itself is "General public services (incl. debt interest)". That label puts debt into one of the top three items on the home page.
- `borrowing.html` opening: "the market rate (yield) for new 10-year UK government borrowing averaged 4.58% a year, **the highest of the G7**. The latest month, September 2026, was 5.24%, **the highest of the G7**." It repeats this in section 2, which has a chart titled "United Kingdom paid the most to borrow for 10 years in 2025". It then gives a prominent OBR quote: "the scale of public borrowing in the UK and other large, advanced economies is putting global sovereign debt markets under pressure."

**What a newcomer takes from it.** Debt interest is one of the biggest calls on public money, and markets are punishing UK borrowing. Both are fiscal-hawk messages. The gross measure is the larger one. The site admits that the OBR and IFS use the lower net figure, and on its own international page it uses net interest for the UK.

**Neutral choice.** Use net debt interest on the home page, consistent with the OBR, IFS and OECD figures used elsewhere, or show both side by side. On the borrowing page, give the yield ranking once and put the Limits point beside it ("different yields can reflect different central bank rates and inflation"), not only in Limits. Balance the OBR "pressure" quote with the DMO's neutral account, or drop it.

## 7. The post-2008 fall in real pay is never worked out, and the headline pay comparison starts in 2021

**Where / exact words.**
- Home and `life/earnings.html`: "After inflation, pay in April 2025 was **about the same as in April 2021**."
- `life/earnings.html`: "From April 2011 to April 2014 it fell (-2.6%). **This page therefore gives no single change from 2008 to 2014.**"
- The highest real figure in the series is £771 (2008), which appears only as a chart scale label ("The scale runs from £712 (2002) to £771 (2008)").

**What a newcomer takes from it.** Pay has been flat recently, and nothing dramatic happened. The best-known pay fact of the period does not appear in any prose sentence: typical full-time real pay in 2025 (£767) is still below its 2008 level (£771), after a long squeeze through the 2010s. That fact cuts against the governments of 2010 to 2024. The break-handling rule is defensible statistically. But ONS itself publishes and comments on the long real-pay comparison, and the site applies its no-crossing rule strictly here while it does join other long series (for example, the 1971 to 2025 employment series is described as "one ONS series").

**Neutral choice.** Say plainly in the earnings lead that real pay in 2025 was about the same as its 2008 peak (with the break caveat), or quote ONS's own words on the long-run comparison, as the site does for other topics.

## 8. The default "compare two years" pair is 2011 versus 2019, which shows improvement on every topic over a single party's decade in office

**Where / exact words.** `life/index.html`: "**Start with 2011 compared with 2019**, the earliest and latest years in which all five topics can be compared". `life/compare/2011-2019.html`, In brief: employment "+5.6 percentage points", pay "+1.8%", holidays abroad "+30%". The "Largest changes" list is all gains: employment of 50 to 64 year olds, women's employment, holidays.

**What a newcomer takes from it.** The obvious first comparison shows the coalition and Conservative years (2010 to 2019) as a period of broad improvement. The start year comes after the 2008 to 2011 falls in pay and employment.

**Neutral choice.** Default to the longest comparable span (2008 versus the latest year), or to the latest year against ten years earlier, or offer no default pair. The site's own methods rule is "fixed windows… so years are not picked for the result". That rule fits a fixed lookback better than a pair chosen because all five topics have figures in both years.

## 9. The biggest welfare totals are put up front: "largest rise was Social protection" and "social security largest"

**Where / exact words.**
- `spending-areas.html`: "Comparing 2003-04 with 2025-26 … **The largest rise was Social protection (£156bn to £407bn)**" (cash). "The largest rise was Social protection (£278bn to £407bn)" (after inflation). Each chart is titled "Social protection was the largest area of spending in every year…".
- `spending-history.html`, opening: "**defence was the largest in 1955-56, and social security in 2019-20**". The chart titles, repeated three times, read "Social security (benefits and State Pension) was the largest of the five in 2021-22".

**What a newcomer takes from it.** Welfare spending dominates and has grown the most. This feeds the "welfare bill out of control" story. Ranking by absolute pounds means the biggest area always shows the "largest rise". When the site ranks by share of GDP, the largest rise is health (5.9% to 8.4%) and social protection is roughly flat (13.6% to 13.3% over the ten-year window on `spending.html`). That appears on a tab, not in the opening.

**Neutral choice.** Rank changes in percentage terms or as a share of GDP, which suit areas of different sizes. Or lead each view with the same measure. Say in the lead sentence that the largest part of social protection is pensions (the site's own "£155bn" old-age pensions line).

## 10. "Tax burden near a post-war high" and the current government's threshold freeze are highlighted

**Where / exact words.**
- `taxes.html`, chart in words: "Receipts: … then rose to 40.1% in 2025-26. **In between: highest 43.3% (1949-50). Forecast for 2031-32: 42.9%.**" Earlier on the page: "a share news reports often call the '**tax burden**'".
- `taxes.html` quote box "Why are more people paying Income Tax?" (HMRC): "frozen Income Tax thresholds … **due to the extension of the freeze at Autumn Budget 2025.**" Fiscal drag also gets a further quote on `household-tax.html` ("creating fiscal drag and so increasing households tax liabilities"), and the threshold freeze appears again in the HMRC quote on `who-pays.html`.

**What a newcomer takes from it.** Taxes are heading back towards their post-war peak, and the latest Budget is pulling more people into tax by stealth. In late 2026 this is the opposition right's main line of attack on the government. The site picks this explanation three times. It quotes no official explanation of what drove spending pressures, such as pension uprating or health demand, on the same pages.

**Neutral choice.** Describe the forecast without pairing it with the 1949-50 record ("Forecast for 2031-32: 42.9%" is enough). Quote one official explanation of the freeze, attributed to the government that introduced it in 2021 as well as to the one that extended it. Balance it with an official explanation of the main spending drivers.

## Weaker candidates not counted in the ten

- `international.html` lead: "Government spending was 46.2% of GDP, **above the average** of 44.5%". This is measured against a simple OECD average pulled down by Mexico, Chile, Colombia and Ireland, whose GDP is inflated. The same page also says UK tax is "close to the OECD average" and second lowest in the G7, which cuts the other way, so I have not counted it.
- `who-pays-age.html`: the ages 65 and over pay "about 13%" of Income Tax, and on who-pays-households 90.1% of people in retired households are "net recipients". This is an intergenerational framing, but it is not clearly left or right.

## Things that cut against my case (for honesty)

The household-tax page leads with lowest and highest fifths paying similar shares of income (36.7% and 36.2%), with most of the lowest fifth's tax being VAT and other indirect taxes. The borrowing page leads with UK debt being "second lowest of the G7". The site quotes the ONS figure that taxes and benefits cut the top-to-bottom income gap from 12.2 times to 3.3 times. Each of these runs against a right-leaning reading.
