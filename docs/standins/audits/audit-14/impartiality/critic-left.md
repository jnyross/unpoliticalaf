# Impartiality critic: the case that the site leans LEFT (audit 14)

A fresh session that did not build the site. I read the built pages in `site/`
(`index.html`, `spending.html`, `taxes.html`, `who-pays.html`,
`household-tax.html`, `life/index.html`, `methods.html`, and pages they link to:
`international.html`, `borrowing.html`, `who-pays-households.html`,
`glossary.html`, `life/housing.html`, `life/earnings.html`,
`life/house-prices.html`, `life/compare/2011-2019.html`). I did not read code,
reviews, other critics, the scorecard or the answer key.

The site is careful overall. Most of what follows is about emphasis, ordering
and which caveat goes where, not false figures. I found ten examples I can
honestly put forward, but only the first five or six are strong. The last few
are weak and I mark them that way.

---

## 1. The poorest fifth is presented as paying the highest share of income in tax (home page and household-tax page)

**Where.** Home page, card "How much household income goes on tax?":
"The lowest-income fifth paid 36.7% of its gross income in tax, and the
highest-income fifth paid 36.2%." The bar list puts the lowest-income fifth
first at 36.7%, the highest value on the chart. The `household-tax.html` page
repeats this: "The share was similar for the lowest-income fifth of people
(36.7%) and the highest-income fifth (36.2%)". The chart heading reads "Taxes
took a similar share of income for the lowest-income fifth and the
highest-income fifth".

**What a newcomer takes away.** The UK tax system is flat or even regressive,
and the poor pay more of their income in tax than the rich. That is a
well-known left talking point ("the poorest pay the highest tax rates").

**Why it is a choice.** The 36.7% depends on dividing indirect tax by gross
income. The site's own table on the same page shows that, measured against
*spending*, indirect tax is 16.5% for the lowest fifth and 15.4% for the
highest, so the gap nearly disappears. The site also quotes the ONS saying low-income
households "may have annual expenditure that exceeds their annual income …
funding their expenditure from savings or borrowing". It mentions that from
2019/20 "lump-sum payments of tax put off from earlier years … can make direct
taxes look high for the lowest-income fifths". All of these caveats are a click
or a scroll away. The home-page headline uses the measure that best fits the
"regressive" story.

**Neutral choice.** Lead with direct taxes, which are clearly progressive
(15.7% to 28.8%), or with the ONS's own headline that taxes and benefits
together cut the top-to-bottom income ratio from 12.2 to 3.3. Or put the
"% of spending" figure next to the 36.7% on the home page.

## 2. Every right-coded statistic comes with a rebuttal attached

**Where.**
- Home page, "Who pays?" card: "The top 1% of Income Tax payers owed 27.2% of
  all Income Tax … **Every household also pays taxes on spending, such as
  VAT.**"
- `who-pays.html` intro: right after the top 1% figure, "The two household
  figures above count all taxes, including VAT and other taxes on spending."
- `taxes.html`: "Taxes alone were 36.0% of GDP in 2025-26, a share news reports
  often call the **“tax burden”**. … it is not anyone's tax bill".
- `glossary.html`, "Tax burden": "This site does not use the name itself … when
  this share goes up, it does not mean everyone pays more".

**What a newcomer takes away.** The facts the right usually cites (high
earners carry most Income Tax; the tax take is high) arrive already qualified.
The scare quotes around "tax burden" and the glossary's "does not mean everyone
pays more" signal that the idea is suspect.

**Why it is a choice.** The left-coded figures get no matching rebuttal on the
home page. Nothing next to the 36.7% card says "most of the lowest fifth's
income is benefits" or "Income Tax is steeply progressive". The 33.6% card's
caveat, "not anyone's tax rate", is neutral, not a counterpoint.

**Neutral choice.** Use the OBR's own term "tax burden" without scare quotes,
with the same caveat style used for other measures. Attach a counterpoint to
both kinds of headline or to neither.

## 3. The high and rising tax take is never described as historically high (taxes page)

**Where.** `taxes.html`, "Receipts and spending since 1948-49": "Receipts: …
fell to a low of 31.2% in 1993-94, then rose to 40.1% in 2025-26. … Forecast for
2031-32: 42.9%." The headline over the chart is "Spending was higher than
receipts in 68 of the 78 years".

**What a newcomer takes away.** That there is nothing unusual about today's tax
level, and that the long-run story is overspending, not taxing.

**Why it is a choice.** Taxes are described by their low point, while spending's
peaks are listed in detail. By the site's own table, receipts of 40.1% are the
highest since 1981-82. The forecast 42.9% would be the highest since 1950-51.
The OBR and most news coverage call this a post-war high. The site describes
other series this way ("highest of the G7", "highest since World War Two" in
the ONS borrowing quote) but not this one.

**Neutral choice.** State it plainly, for example "40.1% of GDP, the highest
since 1981-82; forecast to reach 42.9%, which would be the highest since
1950-51".

## 4. The international comparison sets make the UK look low-tax and low-debt

**Where.**
- `international.html` lead: "UK taxes were 34.4% of GDP, close to the OECD
  average". The G7 panel says "the UK had the second lowest tax as a share of
  GDP". The front G7 spending chart shows only three bars: France 57.3%, UK
  46.2%, US 39.6%.
- `borrowing.html` lead: "UK government debt was 101.3% of GDP, the second
  lowest of the seven G7 countries". The fact that UK debt is above the OECD
  average (92.5% against 77.1%) comes later.
- Home-page link text: "How do UK debt and borrowing costs compare with other
  major countries?"

**What a newcomer takes away.** The UK is a low-tax country with
middling-to-low debt, so there is room to tax and borrow more. That is the
left's case against "tax burden" and "debt crisis" arguments.

**Why it is a choice.** The G7 is a high-debt, high-tax-state peer group
(Japan, Italy, France, the US). Picking it as the lead comparison is what makes
the UK look "second lowest". The OECD average, also on the page, puts the UK
above average on spending and debt. The three-bar spending chart puts France
next to the UK as the visual anchor.

**Neutral choice.** Lead with the OECD average for every measure, or with both
G7 and OECD ranks in the same sentence. Show all G7 bars at the top.

## 5. Debt is described as "not anyone's bill" (taxes page)

**Where.** `taxes.html`, "Borrowing is not the same as debt": "about £41,978
per person (£100,569 per household). That is the debt divided by everyone, an
average, not anyone's bill: **it is owed by the public sector, not by people or
households.**"

**What a newcomer takes away.** Public debt is not really their concern, and
the "every household owes £100k" argument is wrong.

**Why it is a choice.** The debt is serviced and repaid from taxes, which
households pay, as the site itself shows elsewhere. The flat statement that it
is "not owed by people or households" is a contested framing, and it falls on
the side that downplays debt. Compare the per-person *spending* figure, which
gets the milder "averages across everyone".

**Neutral choice.** Keep "an average, not a bill sent to anyone". Drop "it is
owed by the public sector, not by people or households", or add that it is
serviced from public revenue, mainly taxes.

## 6. Housing is framed around the rise in private renting

**Where.**
- `life/housing.html` and the home page: "In 2024-25, 65% of households in
  England owned their home and 19% rented privately. … **In 2008-09, 14%
  rented privately.**"
- `life/compare/*.html`: the housing lead figure on every comparison page is
  "The share of households in England renting privately", not home ownership.
  Its "longer run" line is "2008-09 to 2024-25 … +4.6 percentage points.
  Lowest 14.2% (2008-09)".
- `life/house-prices.html` lead: "the home cost 7.55 times a year's earnings,
  compared with 3.89 times in 1999".

**What a newcomer takes away.** "Generation rent" and a housing affordability
crisis, which are central left themes.

**Why it is a choice.** The only past year named in the housing summary is the
lowest year for private renting in the current series. The summary does not
mention that private renting has *fallen* since its 2016-17 peak (20.3% to
18.9%), or that outright ownership is at a series high (35.5%). The site's own
rule says comparisons use "the first and latest year", and here that happens
to be the most dramatic baseline.

**Neutral choice.** Use home ownership as the compare-page lead, matching the
home-page card. Or state the peak and recent fall next to the 2008-09 baseline.

## 7. The pay chart's headline is the gender pay gap (earnings page)

**Where.** `life/earnings.html`, chart heading "After inflation, the gap
between men's and women's median full-time pay narrowed from £115 a week in
2021 to £105 a week in 2025". The page lead and the home-page card say "pay in
April 2025 was about the same as in April 2021".

**What a newcomer takes away.** Two left-coded frames: pay stagnation, and pay
as a men-versus-women question.

**Why it is a choice.** The chart shows median pay over time. Its title could
describe that, for example "women's pay after inflation rose to its highest on
this measure". The gender gap is one of many possible headlines. The
"about the same as 2021" lead picks a short window that starts at a post-Covid
high point for real pay, caused by a change in workforce composition. The site
gives a reason for that window (job recoding), which partly answers this.

**Neutral choice.** A descriptive chart title ("Median full-time pay after
inflation, men and women"), with the gap given in the text.

## 8. The social protection label puts pensions first (spending page and home page)

**Where.** "Social protection (pensions, benefits, social care) £33". Home page:
"Pensions, benefits and social care take the largest share". Later the page
says "Pensions in old age cost £155bn in 2025-26, inside social protection"
(of £407bn).

**What a newcomer takes away.** The biggest area of spending is mostly
pensions. That makes "welfare" spending seem less open to cuts, which suits
those resisting welfare cuts.

**Why it is a choice.** By the site's own figure, old-age pensions are about
38% of the area, and most of it is something else. Listing pensions first
shapes the impression. (This is a moderate example: HM Treasury's own wording
is similar.)

**Neutral choice.** Order the label by size, or say "benefits, including
pensions (£155bn), and social care".

## 9. ONS and HMRC quotes stress "fiscal drag" and frozen thresholds (taxes and household-tax pages)

**Where.** `taxes.html`, "Why are more people paying Income Tax?", quoting HMRC
on "frozen Income Tax thresholds". `household-tax.html`, "Why did direct taxes
rise in cash in 2023/24?", quoting the ONS: "creating fiscal drag and so
increasing households tax liabilities". `who-pays.html` quotes HMRC that the
fall in top shares "is also due to the freeze".

**What a newcomer takes away.** Ordinary households are being squeezed by
stealth tax rises.

**Why it is a choice.** This is a weak example: the frame is not clearly left,
since threshold freezes have been criticised from every side. The site even
adds a correction that direct taxes did not rise as a share of income. I list
it because of a pattern: the explanatory quotes the site picks tend to be about
pressure on households. There is no matching quote on, for example, why
spending on social protection or health rose.

**Neutral choice.** Explanatory boxes of the same kind for both rises in tax and
rises in spending.

## 10. "Who receives more than they pay" is framed to protect pensioners (who-pays and household pages)

**Where.** `who-pays-households.html`: "retired households … paid less tax on
average, **but they are smaller and have lower incomes**. Compared with each
group's income, the difference is much smaller". The ONS quote then attributes
90.1% net recipients "largely" to "the 10.1% uprating to state pensions".

**What a newcomer takes away.** The low tax paid by retired households is not
a real imbalance.

**Why it is a choice.** This is weak, and not clearly left-coded, since
defending pensioners crosses party lines. The "but" clause defends one group,
and similar groups (for example one adult with children, £9,891) get no such
sentence.

**Neutral choice.** State the amounts and the shares without the "but".

## Counter-evidence (honesty note)

A good deal on the site cuts the other way, and a neutral reader would weigh
this against the list above:
- `who-pays.html` opens with "Higher-income people pay most Income Tax".
- It counts debt interest gross (£11 per £100), not the lower net figure.
- It gives spending per household (£46,895).
- It leads the debt page's second half with "UK 10-year yield … the highest of
  the G7".
- It reports that 53.3% of people are in households receiving more than they
  pay.
- Its spending charts describe 2010-14 only as "rising in most years".

I could not find any false figure or loaded adjective. Examples 1 to 6 are the
real case. Examples 7 to 10 are marginal.
