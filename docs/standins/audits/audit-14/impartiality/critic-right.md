# Impartiality critic: the case that the site leans RIGHT (audit 14)

A fresh session that did not build the site. I read the built pages only: `index.html`, `spending.html`, `spending-areas.html`, `spending-history.html`, `taxes.html`, `tax-history.html`, `who-pays.html`, `who-pays-age.html`, `who-pays-households.html`, `household-tax.html`, `borrowing.html`, `international.html`, `life/index.html`, `life/compare/2011-2019.html` and `methods.html`. I did not read the code, any other critic's output, the scorecard or the answer key.

I found ten examples worth listing. They are listed strongest first. Several are fairly weak on their own: the site usually gives a caveat or a balancing figure somewhere. The case rests on which figure is put first and which window is picked, not on false statements.

---

## 1. "Who pays?" is answered first with the top 1%'s share of Income Tax

**Page:** `index.html` ("Who pays?" card), `who-pays.html` (headline and section 1).

**Exact words / chart:**
- Home page headline number: "**27.2%** The top 1% of Income Tax payers owed 27.2% of all Income Tax in 2023 to 2024. They had 12.4% of all Income Tax payers' income."
- who-pays.html lead sentence: "**Higher-income people pay most Income Tax.**"
- Bar list: "Bottom 50%: share of Income Tax 9.9%".
- Chart title: "**The top 1%'s share of Income Tax was higher than their share of income in every year from 1999-00 to 2023-24**".
- Directly under it, the chosen HMRC quote explains the dips by "a behavioural response by individuals bringing forward, or 'forestalling', income" when the 50% rate came in, and by the cut "from 50% to 45%".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** the rich already carry the system. The bottom half pays almost nothing. Higher top rates mainly make people move their income around. These are standard right-of-centre points against raising taxes on high earners.

**Neutral choice:** answer "Who pays?" first with the all-taxes measure that the site already has. It shows the lowest and highest fifths paying a similar share of income (36.7% and 36.2%). Alternatively, show the HMRC and ONS measures side by side with equal weight. Title the chart neutrally (for example "Top 1% share of Income Tax and of income, 1999-00 to 2023-24"). Put "Income Tax only, about 28% of all tax" next to the headline number, not in a Limits note further down the page. The forestalling quote is the only explanation given, and it is about how people respond to a higher top rate.

## 2. The suggested year comparison is 2011 against 2019, the Coalition and Conservative years

**Page:** `life/index.html` ("Compare two years"), which leads to `life/compare/2011-2019.html`.

**Exact words:** "Start with **2011 compared with 2019**, the earliest and latest years in which all five topics can be compared". The comparison page's brief: employment "went up from 70.5% … to 76.1%", pay "went up from £735 … to £749", holidays abroad "+30%". "Largest changes": employment of 50 to 64 year olds +7.7 points, women +6.8 points, holiday visits +37%.

**What a newcomer takes from it:** the one comparison the site suggests starts at the low point after the recession and ends at the peak before the pandemic. It runs almost exactly through the 2010 to 2019 Coalition and Conservative governments, and almost every headline goes up. The page reads as a record of improvement under those governments. Private renting (+1.2 points) and house prices against earnings (+15%) are there, but further down.

**Neutral choice:** offer no default pair. Or suggest the first and latest year of each series (the rule `methods.html` says it uses elsewhere), or 2008 against the latest year. Do not offer a pair whose two ends match a run of governments.

## 3. "Who receives more in benefits than they pay in taxes?", answered with 53.3%

**Page:** `who-pays.html` section 4, `who-pays-households.html`.

**Exact words:** "**In 2023/24, 53.3% of people lived in households that received more in benefits and services than they paid in taxes.** That was 90.1% of people in retired households and 45.8% in non-retired households." The chart title stresses that the retired share was higher "in every year from 1977". The ONS quote on the household page uses the term "net recipients".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** most people take out more than they put in. That is the right-of-centre "makers and takers" framing, and it would be used to argue that the welfare state is too large. The Limits note says the measure counts the NHS and state schools as benefits and covers one year only, and that the same person is on both sides over a lifetime. But that comes after the headline percentage.

**Neutral choice:** ask the question as "How do taxes and benefits move money between groups?" Lead with the redistribution figure the site quotes elsewhere (gap of 12.2 times before taxes and benefits, 3.3 times after). Put the lifetime and NHS caveats in the headline sentence itself.

## 4. An old ONS explanation is brought back to blunt the point that VAT is regressive

**Page:** `household-tax.html`, under "Taxes as a share of gross income, by household income".

**Exact words:** after showing that indirect tax is 21.0% of the lowest fifth's gross income, the site asks "Why can indirect taxes be a large share of income for lower-income households?" It answers with ONS text: these households "may maintain their standard of living by funding their expenditure from savings or borrowing, thereby adjusting their lifetime consumption". The site itself says this is from the 2016 bulletin and that "the latest ONS bulletin does not repeat this explanation". The page's lead sentence calls the bottom and top fifth shares "similar" (36.7% and 36.2%). The bottom fifth's figure is in fact the highest of the five.

**What a newcomer takes from it:** the high tax share at the bottom is a statistical quirk of people spending savings, not evidence that taxes fall hardest on the poor. Picking a ten-year-old explanation that the publisher has since dropped, to answer the one finding most useful to the left on this page, is a selective choice.

**Neutral choice:** use the latest bulletin's own wording, or no quote. Describe the fifths in order ("the lowest-income fifth paid the largest share, 36.7%, the highest-income fifth 36.2%") rather than "similar".

## 5. The debt page leads with "UK borrowing costs are the highest in the G7" and blames borrowing

**Page:** `borrowing.html` (and `index.html` link text).

**Exact words:** "the market rate (yield) for new 10-year UK government borrowing averaged 4.58% a year, **the highest of the G7**. The latest month, September 2026, was 5.24%, **the highest of the G7**." The chart title is "United Kingdom paid the most to borrow for 10 years in 2025". The chosen OBR quote: "There are signs that **the scale of public borrowing** in the UK and other large, advanced economies is putting global sovereign debt markets under pressure … long-term gilt yields are now higher in the UK than at any point since the start of the century." The summary also gives net interest as "the third highest of the G7".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** the markets are punishing UK borrowing, so the government must cut the deficit. This is the fiscal-discipline argument associated with the right. The point that yields mainly follow central bank rates and inflation appears only in Limits ("Different yields can reflect different central bank rates and inflation").

**Neutral choice:** keep that caveat in the headline sentence. Show real yields, or Bank Rate alongside the yields. Give equal weight to the fact that UK debt is "the second lowest of the seven G7 countries". The page does say this, but the yield figure gets two "highest" mentions and a chart title. Choose explanatory quotes that list causes, not one that names "the scale of public borrowing".

## 6. Pay is compared from 2021, which hides that real pay is still below 2008

**Page:** `index.html` (Earnings card), `life/index.html` (Earnings).

**Exact words:** "After inflation, pay in April 2025 was **about the same as in April 2021**." The small chart caption says 2025 is "£767, **the highest of the 5 years measured the same way**". The same caption says "The scale runs from £712 (2002) to **£771 (2008)**".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** pay is stable, and at a recent high. The stronger finding, that typical real full-time pay in 2025 is still below its 2008 level after 17 years, is not stated. It can only be worked out from a scale note. That "lost decade" of pay is one of the main charges against the governments in power from 2010 to 2024.

**Neutral choice:** keep the break in coding, but add a plain sentence such as: "Across the coding changes, the highest figure was in 2008 (£771); 2025 is £767." Avoid "highest of the 5 years", which uses the method break to produce a favourable superlative.

## 7. The "everyday life" topics paint an upbeat picture

**Page:** `life/index.html`, `index.html` ("Everyday life, year by year").

**Exact words:** the five topics are work, earnings, housing (led by home ownership), **holidays abroad** and class sizes. The captions read "75.3%, higher than in 51 of the other 54 years shown"; visits abroad "126, higher than in 40 of the other 43 years shown"; home ownership "65%, higher than in 10 of the other 16 years". Holidays lead with "58.4 million holiday visits abroad".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** life in Britain is good: record employment, record foreign holidays, high home ownership. There is nothing on poverty, food bank use, homelessness or temporary accommodation, NHS waiting times, or child poverty. Those are the everyday-life measures the left points to. Holidays abroad is an unusual choice as a measure of living standards, and it nearly always rises.

**Neutral choice:** choose topics with a stated rule and include at least one measure of hardship (for example relative or absolute low income from HBAI, or NHS referral-to-treatment waits), so the set does not move only in one direction. Lead housing with tenure as a whole, not "owned their home".

## 8. Spending "rose … in most years", and the ten-year window starts after the austerity cuts

**Page:** `spending.html`.

**Exact words:** "After adjusting for rising prices, **total spending in 2025-26 was 54% higher than in 2003-04**." The chart summary for after inflation reads "Rose from £882bn in 2003-04 to £1,360bn in 2025-26, **rising in most years**." The change by area uses "2015-16 to 2025-26 … The two years are a fixed ten years apart, not chosen for the result". Nine of ten areas show a rise (health +34%, general public services +56%, housing +64%).

**What a newcomer takes from it:** the state keeps getting bigger and no service has been cut. Real spending in the table was almost flat from 2010-11 (£1,116bn) to 2019-20 (£1,132bn) while the population grew. Education after inflation went from £127bn (2011-12) to £126bn (`spending-areas.html`). Neither of these is said in the headline. A window that starts in 2015-16 begins after most of the 2010 to 2015 cuts. However fixed the rule, its result removes the austerity years from view.

**Neutral choice:** describe the after-inflation series in phases, as the share-of-GDP summary already does ("held roughly level from 2010-11 to 2019-20"). Add real spending per person. Show the area change from 2009-10 or 2010-11 as well, so the period of cuts is visible.

## 9. Spending, tax and debt are turned into "per household" bills

**Page:** `spending.html`, `taxes.html`.

**Exact words:** "about **£46,895 per household**, or £902 a week"; "Borrowing was £1,933 per person, or **£4,630 per household**"; debt "about £41,978 per person (**£100,569 per household**)".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** every household is in effect paying £47,000 a year for the state and "owes" £100,000 of national debt. This is the framing used by campaigns for lower taxes and smaller government. The site adds "not anyone's bill", but the six-figure number is the one people remember.

**Neutral choice:** use shares of GDP, which the site uses elsewhere. Or, if per-head figures are wanted, show them in a table rather than in running text and next to per-household GDP or income for scale.

## 10. Fiscal-alarm framing: gross debt interest and the "68 of 78 years" headline

**Page:** `index.html`, `spending.html`, `taxes.html`.

**Exact words:** home page: "General public services take £13, of which **£11 is interest paid on debt, counted before interest received**. The OBR and IFS often quote a lower figure that subtracts interest received." `taxes.html` chart title: "**Spending was higher than receipts in 68 of the 78 years from 1948-49 to 2025-26**." The page also has "Why are more people paying Income Tax?" (frozen thresholds).

**What a newcomer takes from it:** debt interest is a large and growing drain, and governments almost always overspend. Both points lead towards deficit reduction. The site chose the gross measure of interest even though it says the standard measure used by the UK's official forecaster and the IFS is lower.

**Neutral choice:** lead with net debt interest, the OBR and IFS measure, and give the gross figure as the alternative. Title the long-run chart with its shape (for example "Receipts and spending as a share of GDP, 1948-49 to 2025-26") rather than a count of deficit years.

---

**Overall:** none of these is a false statement, and most carry a caveat somewhere on the page. The pattern that would push a newcomer to the right is in the choices that come first: the top 1% headline on "Who pays?"; the 2011 to 2019 default comparison; pay compared from 2021; yields "highest of the G7" with an OBR quote that blames borrowing; benefits "net recipients"; and per-household bills. The balancing figures (similar tax shares across fifths, UK debt second lowest in the G7, flat real spending in the 2010s) are usually there, but with less prominence.
