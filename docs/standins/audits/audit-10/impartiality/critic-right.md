# The case that the site leans right

Reviewer stance: a critic trying to show that the built site nudges a newcomer towards right-leaning conclusions. I read only the built HTML pages (site version 10255dc): home, spending, spending-areas, spending-history, taxes, tax-history, who-pays (and its households and age sub-pages), household-tax, borrowing, international, methods, glossary, life/index, the life topic pages and the 2011–2019 comparison page. I did not read the code, `docs/` or any earlier review.

To be fair to the site first: it is unusually careful. It caveats almost every number, quotes official bodies word for word instead of explaining things itself, and often gives the opposite figure as well (for example, the top 1%'s share of income sits beside their share of Income Tax). So none of the examples below is a false statement. The case rests on **what comes first, what gets the headline, which years frame things, and which official quotes were picked**. Taken together, those choices tell a fairly consistent centre-right story: the rich already carry the tax system, the system is flat or fair at the bottom, most people take out more than they put in, spending and debt are large and growing, bond markets are passing judgement, and everyday life improved through the 2010s.

Strongest first.

---

## 1. The suggested first comparison is 2011 vs 2019, which happens to be the Coalition and Conservative decade, and every lead figure goes up

**Page:** `life/index.html`, then `life/compare/2011-2019.html`.

**Exact words:** "Start with **2011 compared with 2019**, the earliest and latest years in which all five topics can be compared". The comparison page then opens with "Work … went up from 70.5% … to 76.1% … +5.6 percentage points", "Median full-time weekly pay, after inflation, went up from £735 (April 2011) to £749 (April 2019). Change: +1.8%", "Holiday visits abroad … went up from 68 … to 88 … +30%". Under "Largest changes between 2011 and 2019" the money section is led by "Holiday visits abroad … (+37%)".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** the one comparison the site puts forward runs almost exactly over the years of Conservative-led government (2010–2019). It starts near the low point of employment after the financial crisis and ends at the record high (76.1%, which the page itself calls the highest). Jobs, pay and holidays all rise. The newcomer concludes that life got better over the 2010s.

**Why it leans:** the reason given ("earliest and latest years in which all five topics can be compared") rests on the site's own choice to break the pay series at 2011 and 2021. 2008 or 2010 would show real pay falling (£771 in 2008, £764 in 2010, then £735 in 2011). Ending in 2019 skips the later years. Holidays abroad are an odd lead indicator of living standards, and here they produce the biggest "money" rise.

**Neutral choice:** suggest no starting pair, or suggest one that is not a political era (for example the first and latest year of each series, as the methods page says it uses elsewhere). At the least, say that 2011 sits just after a recession trough.

## 2. "Who pays?" is answered first, and on the home page only, with the top 1%'s share of Income Tax

**Pages:** `index.html` (the "Who pays?" card) and `who-pays.html` (hero figure and section 1).

**Exact words:** home: "**27.2%** of all Income Tax was paid by the top 1% of Income Tax payers". Who pays, opening sentence: "Higher-income people pay most Income Tax." Section 1 chart: "Top 1% … 27.2% · Top 10% … 59.1% · Top 50% … 90.1% · Bottom 50% … 9.9%". The over-time chart title: "The top 1%'s share of Income Tax was higher than their share of income in every year".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** a small group of high earners carries the tax system, and the bottom half pays almost nothing (9.9%). That is the standard argument against taxing the rich more.

**Why it leans:** Income Tax is the most progressive tax and raises about 30% of all tax. The site's own household-tax page shows that across all taxes the lowest-income fifth pays a larger share of its income (36.7%) than the highest-income fifth (36.2%). The HMRC figures also leave out everyone who owes no Income Tax, so "bottom 50%" means the bottom half of taxpayers, not of people. The order of sections (Income Tax by person first, all taxes by household second) and the home-page card both put the most progressive slice of the system up front. (In fairness, the home card does add "They had 12.4% of all Income Tax payers' income.")

**Neutral choice:** lead "Who pays?" with all taxes as a share of income by fifth, which covers everyone and every tax. Or show the 27.2% and the 36.7%/36.2% side by side with equal weight on the home page.

## 3. The household-tax page frames the system as flat, and uses an old ONS quote to explain away the higher burden on the poorest

**Page:** `household-tax.html`.

**Exact words:**
- Lead: "The share was **similar** for the lowest-income fifth of people (36.7%) and the highest-income fifth (36.2%)."
- The ONS quote picked to answer "Why can indirect taxes be a large share of income for lower-income households?": "…their expenditure is not being funded entirely from income … these households may maintain their standard of living by funding their expenditure from savings or borrowing, thereby adjusting their lifetime consumption." The site itself notes: "This is from the ONS bulletin for April 2015 to March 2016 … the latest ONS bulletin does not repeat this explanation."
- "Why the same tax can look bigger or smaller": a table showing that indirect taxes as a share of *spending* are roughly flat (16.5% for the lowest fifth, 15.4% for the highest).
- A second ONS quote stressing how much taxes and benefits cut the gap between rich and poor: "12.2 times larger … reduced to 3.3 times larger".
- Chart title: "Taxes took a similar share of income for the lowest-income fifth and the highest-income fifth in 2023/24".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** the system is roughly flat. The high indirect-tax share for poorer households is a measurement quirk to do with temporary low income and savings. And the system already redistributes heavily. Each of these points weakens the case for more progressive taxes.

**Why it leans:** the poorest fifth's share is in fact the highest of all five fifths. Calling it "similar" plays that down. The site went back to a nine-year-old bulletin for an explanation that excuses the pattern, when the current publisher no longer offers it. It then added an extra view (share of spending) that makes the pattern disappear. There is no matching quote or view on the other side, for example on how regressive VAT is.

**Neutral choice:** "The share was highest for the lowest-income fifth (36.7%), slightly above the highest-income fifth (36.2%)." Quote only the current bulletin, or give the lifetime-consumption point alongside a mainstream statement that indirect taxes take a larger share of income from poorer households.

## 4. The breaks in the earnings series hide that real pay in 2025 was below 2008, and the home page calls 2025 "the highest"

**Pages:** `index.html` (Earnings card), `life/index.html`, `life/earnings.html`.

**Exact words:** "The dark dot is 2025: £767, **the highest of the 5 years measured the same way**. The scale runs from £717 (2014) to **£771 (2008)**". Earnings page lead: "After allowing for rising prices, that was about the same as in April 2021". Changes are given only within short stretches: "+1.1% … +1.0% … +0.8%".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** real pay is at a high and has grown a little in every period. The idea that pay has stagnated since 2008, one of the most common left-of-centre criticisms of the 2010s, never comes up.

**Why it leans:** the site's own table shows £771 in 2008 and £767 in 2025, both in 2025 prices. The breaks are changes in how jobs are coded, which ONS treats as minor for the overall median. Because no change is ever shown across a break, there is never a "17 years with no pay rise" figure, and the small chart's top value (2008) sits above a dot labelled "the highest".

**Neutral choice:** keep the breaks, but say plainly in the lead that the 2025 figure is "slightly below 2008 (£771), although the survey's job coding changed in between". Or drop "the highest of the 5 years" from the label.

## 5. Debt interest gets a hero spot on the spending page, using the larger gross figure

**Pages:** `spending.html` (hero, lead and table notes), `index.html` (spending card).

**Exact words:** hero: "**£11 of every £100 went on debt interest**, part of general public services". Home: "Debt interest (£11) is inside general public services." The limits note: "Interest on government debt here is the interest paid out … The OBR and the IFS often quote 'net' debt interest … so their figure for the same year is lower."

**What a newcomer takes from it:** debt is eating a large share of the budget, so the country cannot afford to borrow or spend more. That is the core message of fiscal conservatives.

**Why it leans:** debt interest is not one of the site's own COFOG spending areas. It is pulled out of "general public services" and given one of only three hero slots, ahead of education (£10), which is an area. The site picks the gross figure, which is higher, although it admits the official forecaster and the main think tank use the net one. On the borrowing page the UK's net interest is 2.2% of GDP.

**Neutral choice:** hero slots for the top areas only (social protection, health, education), with debt interest in the body text. Or show gross and net together.

## 6. Debt and borrowing shown per person and per household, and deficits made the headline of the long-run chart

**Page:** `taxes.html`.

**Exact words:** "£2,917bn … or about **£41,978 per person (£100,569 per household)**". "Borrowing was £1,933 per person, or **£4,630 per household**." Chart title: "**Spending was higher than receipts in 68 of the 78 years** from 1948-49 to 2025-26" (and "65 of the 71 years" in the after-inflation view). Hero: "£134bn borrowed to cover spending above receipts". Borrowing "adds to the national debt".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** every household is in effect £100,000 in debt, and governments almost always overspend. That is the household-budget picture of public finances that fiscal conservatives use.

**Why it leans:** the caveat ("owed by the public sector, not by people or households") comes after the striking figure, which has already landed. Debt per household is a well-known rhetorical device. Of all the facts in the receipts-and-spending chart, the title picks the deficit count. It could as easily have picked what the chart is mainly about, the size of the state over time. No similar per-household figure is given for public assets, or for spending on services received.

**Neutral choice:** give debt as a share of GDP only (as the site does on the borrowing page), and title the chart with its main pattern (for example "Receipts and spending have both stayed between about 31% and 53% of GDP since 1948").

## 7. "53.3% of people … received more in benefits than they paid in taxes"

**Page:** `who-pays.html`, section 4 (also `who-pays-households.html`).

**Exact words:** section heading: "Who receives more in benefits than they pay in taxes?" Text: "In 2023/24, **53.3%** of people lived in households that received more in benefits and services than they paid in taxes." Next to it, the series rising from "37.0% in 1977".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** most people are now net takers, and dependency has grown over time. This is the "makers and takers" framing.

**Why it leans:** this is one of only five sections on the main "Who pays?" page, and it frames the question in terms of net contributors and net recipients. The life-cycle point (children in school, retired people drawing pensions they paid for) is in the "Limits" box, below the headline. The figure counts the NHS and schools as "benefits", but the section is headed with that word, which a newcomer reads as welfare.

**Neutral choice:** leave this off the main page, or open with the life-cycle point ("Most people receive more than they pay as children and in retirement, and pay more than they receive while working") before the percentage. Title it "benefits and public services".

## 8. The borrowing page leads with the UK's bond yields being "the highest of the G7" and picks an OBR quote blaming the scale of borrowing

**Page:** `borrowing.html`.

**Exact words:** lead: "In September 2026, the UK government paid **5.24%** a year to borrow for 10 years, **the highest of the G7**." "There is no single official measure of how easy it is for a government to borrow more. **The yield investors ask for is the closest one**." OBR quote: "There are signs that **the scale of public borrowing** in the UK and other large, advanced economies is putting global sovereign debt markets under pressure … long-term gilt yields are now higher in the UK than at any point since the start of the century." Chart title: "The UK's net interest bill was higher than the OECD average in every year from 2007 to 2024".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** markets are judging the UK harshly and too much borrowing is the cause, so borrowing must come down. That is the "bond vigilantes" argument for fiscal restraint.

**Why it leans:** the lead uses a single recent month. "The yield investors ask for is the closest one" is the site's own sentence, and it presents market yields as the measure of how much a government can borrow. Of the three quotes, the one that gives a cause names borrowing. To be fair, the page also says the UK has the second-lowest debt in the G7 and that yields are "not … a verdict on how well a country is run". Still, the headline and the quote both point the same way.

**Neutral choice:** use the yearly average (already shown) instead of a single month. Cut the "closest measure of how easy it is to borrow" sentence. Pick a quote that covers several causes (the DMO quote already does this) rather than one that names borrowing.

## 9. How spending growth is framed: a 2015-16 baseline and real totals that are not per person, with "Social protection" as "the largest rise"

**Pages:** `spending.html`, `spending-areas.html`, `index.html`.

**Exact words:** "After adjusting for rising prices, total spending in 2025-26 was **54% higher** than in 2003-04." "Rose from £882bn … to £1,360bn …, **rising in most years**." Ten-year bars: every area but one shows a "rise" (Health 34%, General public services 56%, Housing 64% …). On spending-areas: "**The largest rise was Social protection** (£156bn to £407bn)". Repeated chart titles: "Social protection was the largest area of spending in every year".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** the state keeps growing in real terms, in nearly every area, and welfare is the biggest and fastest-growing part. They see no sign of austerity.

**Why it leans:** totals are never shown per person, although the population grew by millions over these years. The fixed ten-year window starts in 2015-16, after most of the 2010–15 cuts, so it shows recovery but not the cuts. "Largest rise" is in pounds, which always favours the biggest area. And social protection is mostly pensions, yet it gets the attention as "the largest".

**Neutral choice:** add real spending per person. Give the ten-year window together with the change since 2009-10 or 2010-11. Rank rises in per cent, or say plainly that pensions make up most of social protection wherever that area gets top billing.

## 10. Comparing with other countries: taxes are "close to" the average, spending is "above" it

**Page:** `international.html`.

**Exact words:** "UK taxes were 34.4% of GDP, **close to** the OECD average of 34.1%. Government spending was 46.2% of GDP, **above** the average of 44.5%."

**What a newcomer takes from it:** the UK taxes like a normal country but spends more than one, so the gap points to overspending rather than undertaxing.

**Why it leans:** the comparison group is the OECD's simple average, which includes Mexico, Colombia, Chile, Costa Rica and Turkey, all with low taxes. Against the countries the UK usually compares itself with, it is a low-tax country: second lowest in the G7, and below almost all of Western Europe, which the site does show further down. The lead picks the comparison that makes UK spending look high and UK tax look ordinary.

**Neutral choice:** describe both with the same kind of words ("0.3 points above the average" and "1.7 points above the average"), and give the G7 or EU position in the lead alongside the OECD one.

### Weaker examples I left out

- The life topics (work, pay, home ownership, holidays abroad, class sizes) leave out poverty, food insecurity and NHS waiting times. Holidays abroad as a measure of everyday life gives a fairly rosy picture. This is a matter of topic selection rather than wording, so I folded it into example 1.
- The home page Housing card opens with "65% … owned their home" before private renting. This is a mild positive framing; the small chart does track private renting.
- The taxes page names the "tax burden" phrase. It is attributed to news reports and explained neutrally in the glossary, so I do not count it.

Ten examples found. The first four are the strongest. Examples 8 to 10 are each partly balanced by caveats on the same page.
