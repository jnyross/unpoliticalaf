# Critic: does the site lean left?

Audit 10, impartiality. The brief was to make the strongest honest case that the built site nudges a newcomer towards left-leaning conclusions in UK politics. I read only the built HTML pages: `index.html`, `spending.html`, `spending-areas.html`, `spending-history.html`, `taxes.html`, `tax-history.html`, `who-pays.html`, `who-pays-households.html`, `who-pays-age.html`, `household-tax.html`, `borrowing.html`, `international.html`, `glossary.html`, `methods.html`, `life/index.html`, every topic page under `life/`, plus `life/2010.html` and `life/compare/1979-2025.html` as samples. I did not read the code, `docs/`, or any earlier review.

**Honest framing first.** The site is careful. Nearly every figure carries a caveat, and several choices lean the other way: the top-1% Income Tax share is a homepage headline, debt interest is called out at £11 in every £100, and the 2015-16 to 2025-26 window on the spending page shows every area rising, which hides the 2010s cuts. So the case below is about emphasis, ordering and framing, not invented numbers. I found ten examples worth listing. The last two are clearly weaker than the first six.

---

## 1. "The poorest pay as big a share in tax as the richest", on the homepage, with the caveats a click away

**Page:** `index.html` (card "How much household income goes on tax?") and `household-tax.html`.

**Exact words:** the homepage lists the fifths with the lowest first: "Lowest-income fifth 36.7% … Highest-income fifth 36.2%", then: "For the lowest-income fifth, most of this is indirect tax such as VAT ( 21.0% of income)". `household-tax.html` opens with: "The share was similar for the lowest-income fifth of people ( 36.7% ) and the highest-income fifth ( 36.2% )". Its chart title reads: "Taxes took a similar share of income for the lowest-income fifth and the highest-income fifth in 2023/24, less than one percentage point apart".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** the tax system is not progressive, and may even be regressive at the bottom. The poor pay at least as large a share as the rich, because of VAT. This is a well-known campaigning line on the left (Equality Trust, TUC-style "the poorest pay the most tax").

**Why it nudges:** the ONS's own warning appears only in a collapsed "Why can indirect taxes be a large share…" box on the second page. That warning is that low-income households often spend more than their income for a while, from savings or borrowing, which inflates their indirect-tax ratio. The site then weakens the warning further by adding: "This is from the ONS bulletin for April 2015 to March 2016 … and the latest ONS bulletin does not repeat this explanation." The second caveat sits in the Limits list at the bottom of the page: deferred lump-sum tax payments "can make direct taxes look high for the lowest-income fifths". The homepage card shows neither caveat. The comparison on a spending basis is published on the same page, and there indirect taxes are almost flat (16.5% for the lowest fifth, 15.4% for the highest). It is filed under "Why the same tax can look bigger or smaller", not shown in the headline.

**Neutral choice:** lead with pounds (the £8,625 against £51,903 already on `who-pays.html`), or with direct taxes as a share of income. Or keep the gross-income shares but put the ONS's spending-exceeds-income caveat, and the deferred-tax caveat, in the same card on the homepage. Do not label the ONS caveat as stale.

## 2. The homepage answers "the top 1% pay 27%" with a rebuttal line

**Page:** `index.html`, "Who pays?" card.

**Exact words:** "27.2% of all Income Tax was paid by the top 1% of Income Tax payers in 2023 to 2024. They had 12.4% of all Income Tax payers' income. **Every household also pays taxes on spending, such as VAT.**"

**What a newcomer takes from it:** the top-1% figure is misleading, because everyone pays tax and the poor pay through VAT. The next card down ("Lowest-income fifth 36.7%", example 1) then seems to confirm it. The two cards together read as the argument "the rich don't really carry the load".

**Why it nudges:** none of the other homepage cards adds a "but" sentence of this kind. No card on spending says "every household also uses the NHS and schools", and the debt figure carries no line like "every household will also pay this back through future taxes". Only the statistic that is most often quoted against higher taxes on the rich gets a counterweight.

**Neutral choice:** drop the sentence, or give each card the same kind of qualifier. If the point about indirect tax stays, pair it with the pounds figure from `who-pays.html` (highest fifth £51,903, lowest £8,625).

## 3. "Who gets more than they pay" is split by retired against non-retired, never by income

**Page:** `who-pays.html`, section 4, and `who-pays-households.html`.

**Exact words:** "In 2023/24, 53.3% of people lived in households that received more in benefits and services than they paid in taxes. That was 90.1% of people in retired households and 45.8% in non-retired households." The chart compares "Retired" with "Non-retired" from 1977.

**What a newcomer takes from it:** pensioners are the people who take out more than they put in. The question of whether lower-income households are net recipients and higher-income households net contributors is never answered.

**Why it nudges:** the same ONS release (*Effects of taxes and benefits*) publishes net benefits and taxes by income fifth. That table shows large net transfers from the top fifth to the bottom fifths, which is the figure most often used to argue that the system is already highly redistributive. The site shows the split by retirement and leaves out the split by income. The only nod is an ONS quote ("12.2 times larger … reduced to 3.3 times"), inside a collapsed box on a different page (`household-tax.html`). Splitting by age also moves the "who takes out" question onto generations, a framing popular on the centre-left, and away from the welfare system.

**Neutral choice:** show the net position (benefits in cash and in kind minus taxes) for each income fifth beside the retired and non-retired split, from the same ONS table.

## 4. UK debt is introduced as "second lowest of the G7"

**Page:** `borrowing.html`, the opening sentence and the first chart.

**Exact words:** "In 2024, UK government debt was 101.3% of GDP, the second lowest of the seven G7 countries: lower than Japan, Italy, the United States, France and Canada and higher than Germany."

**What a newcomer takes from it:** UK debt is moderate by international standards, so there is room to borrow more. This is a common argument on the left against fiscal restraint.

**Why it nudges:** the G7 is the most indebted group of rich countries (Japan 237%, Italy 135%, US 121%, France 113%). It is the comparison group that makes 101% look low. The same page also has the OECD average (77.1%), which puts the UK well above the typical rich country, but that comes second and below the fold. The site's own IMF table shows UK debt going from 28.3% of GDP in 1990 to 101.3% in 2024, the second-largest rise in the G7 after Japan. That figure is not in the headline. The chart's summary line names only Japan as "the largest rise".

**Neutral choice:** headline the UK against the OECD average, or against its own history ("up from 28.3% in 1990"). Or give both rankings in the same sentence, with the OECD ranking first.

## 5. The unfavourable borrowing figure gets an excuse that the favourable one does not

**Page:** `borrowing.html`, "How much does each government pay to borrow?" and "What affects…".

**Exact words:** just before the yields chart, which shows the UK highest of the G7 at 5.24%: "Borrowing costs depend on central bank interest rates, expected inflation, how much a government borrows and how much risk investors see. They are not, on their own, a verdict on how well a country is run." Later, a question heading: "**Have borrowing costs risen only in the UK?**", answered with an OBR quote that starts with "global sovereign debt markets under pressure".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** the high UK yield is a global phenomenon, not a judgement on UK fiscal policy.

**Why it nudges:** the debt ranking, which flatters the UK, gets no warning that it is "not a verdict". The reassuring caveat appears only where the figure looks bad. The question heading primes the answer "no, it's everyone", even though the OBR passage it quotes ends by saying UK long-term yields are "higher … than at any point since the start of the century". The euro-area note in Limits also steers the reader away from reading the UK–Germany gap as investor judgement.

**Neutral choice:** put one symmetrical note under both charts, for example "neither debt nor yields alone shows whether a level is right". Use a neutral heading such as "How have other countries' borrowing costs moved?"

## 6. "Close to the OECD average" and "second lowest tax in the G7"

**Page:** `international.html`, opening sentence and G7 table.

**Exact words:** "In 2024, UK taxes were 34.4% of GDP, close to the OECD average of 34.1%." And: "the UK had the second lowest tax as a share of GDP in 2024 (of the 6 with a figure for that year)".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** UK taxes are average or low, so there is room to raise them. This argument is made mostly from the left.

**Why it nudges:** the OECD simple average counts Mexico (18.3%), Colombia, Chile, Costa Rica and Turkey equally with Germany and France. Against Western European neighbours, the UK is below the middle, but against the countries it is usually compared with in UK debate it reads as "average". The G7 rank is out of six, because Japan is missing, and the US at 25.6% is in the group. The headline also uses the OECD's 34.4%, while the site's own taxes page gives 36.0% for 2025-26 on the domestic measure, so a reader who sees both comes away thinking the lower figure is the comparable one. The site does say why the measures differ, but only in Limits.

**Neutral choice:** state the UK's rank among all 36 countries in the opening sentence (the page already has it: "21 collected a larger share"). Add the UK's own trend: the site's OECD table shows 29.1% in 1996 against 34.4% now. Do not lead with "close to the average".

## 7. The "tax burden" is held at arm's length, and its near-record level is never stated

**Pages:** `taxes.html` and `glossary.html`.

**Exact words:** `taxes.html`: "Taxes alone were 36.0% of GDP in 2025-26, a share news reports often call the 'tax burden'. It compares all tax … so it is not anyone's tax bill". `glossary.html`: "'Tax burden' … This site does not use the name itself … when this share goes up, it does not mean everyone pays more".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** worry about a rising tax burden is a media framing, and a high tax-to-GDP figure doesn't really affect anyone.

**Why it nudges:** the phrase is the standard term used by the OBR and IFS, not only by the right-leaning press. The site puts it in quotation marks and attaches a "doesn't mean you pay more" disclaimer. There is no matching disclaimer when spending as a share of GDP rises. The site's own receipts table also shows receipts forecast to reach 42.9% of GDP in 2031-32, above every year since 1949-50. The chart's summary line reports the forecast but never says how high it is in historical terms. Compare borrowing: there the site quotes the ONS saying the 2020-21 deficit was "the highest such ratio since the end of World War Two". The "highest since…" framing is used for borrowing, which can be read as a reason for crisis spending, and not for tax.

**Neutral choice:** use the OBR's term without quotation marks or a disclaimer, as with any other official label. State the historical position plainly: "the forecast for 2031-32 would be the highest share since 1949-50".

## 8. Deficits are made to look normal, and debt is said not to be owed by people

**Page:** `taxes.html`.

**Exact words:** chart title: "Spending was higher than receipts in 68 of the 78 years from 1948-49 to 2025-26". And: "Debt … £2,917bn … about £41,978 per person ( £100,569 per household). That is the debt divided by everyone, an average, not anyone's bill: **it is owed by the public sector, not by people or households.**"

**What a newcomer takes from it:** running a deficit is the normal state of affairs, and the national debt is not really their concern.

**Why it nudges:** of all the things that chart could headline (the 2025-26 gap, the forecast, the post-2008 rise in debt), the site picked the one that normalises borrowing. The debt sentence goes further than a caveat. Public debt is serviced and repaid out of future taxes paid by people and households, so "not owed by people" steers the reader towards a well-known argument against fiscal restraint. The per-person tax figure gets a comparable "not anyone's bill" disclaimer, but the per-person spending figures on `spending.html` get only a milder "averages across everyone".

**Neutral choice:** headline the chart with the latest gap or the long-run shape. Replace the debt sentence with something factual both ways: "it is owed by the public sector, and its interest is paid from public revenue."

## 9. Official explanations that point to Conservative-era policy, and an inequality lens on pay

**Pages:** `life/earnings.html`, `household-tax.html`.

**Exact words:** the only explanation offered for weak pay after 2008 is the ONS saying: "In the public sector, a pay freeze (from 2011) and pay cap (from 2013) kept wage rises below inflation". On `household-tax.html`, the only explanation for rising direct taxes is "Income tax thresholds in FYE 2024 remained at 2022 levels, creating fiscal drag". The earnings page's chart titles headline gaps rather than levels: "the gap between men's and women's median full-time pay narrowed…" and "the gap between the top-tenth and bottom-tenth lines narrowed…".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** stagnant pay and higher tax bills come from specific decisions taken by the 2010–2024 governments (the pay freeze and cap, the frozen thresholds), and the main story in pay is about inequality.

**Why it nudges:** the site's rule is to quote official explanations rather than give its own. But it chooses which explanation to quote, and both name a Conservative-led policy. ONS and OBR material just as often explains weak post-2008 pay by weak productivity growth, and none of that is quoted. Making gender and 90/10 gaps the chart headlines is a choice of lens, not a property of the data.

**Neutral choice:** where an official explanation names a policy, pair it with the publisher's broader explanation (productivity, the financial crisis). Headline the pay charts with the level ("median pay after inflation in 2025 was about the same as in 2021"), as the page's opening already does.

## 10. Housing told as "generation rent" (weaker)

**Pages:** `life/index.html`, `index.html`, `life/housing.html`, `life/house-prices.html`.

**Exact words:** the small chart chosen for housing on `life/index.html` is "Households renting privately, 2008-09 to 2025-26 … higher than in 9 of the other 16 years", not home ownership. The first chart title on the housing page is "the share renting privately rose most and the share buying with a mortgage fell most". The quotes chosen include "71% of private renters in the lowest two income quintiles spent more than 30% of their gross household income on rent" and the share of first-time buyers on 30-year mortgages. Rents are "before housing support". The house-price ratio runs from "3.55 times in 1997", a low point after the early-1990s crash.

**What a newcomer takes from it:** a housing-affordability crisis in which private renting is spreading and becoming unaffordable. This is the framing used by renters' campaigns and the centre-left.

**Why it is weaker:** worry about affordability is cross-party, and 1997 is the first year of the ONS series, not a pick. But the small chart starts at 2008-09, the private-renting low (14.2%), so the line can only go up. And rents shown before Housing Benefit, with no figure for housing support at all, leave out the state's biggest response.

**Neutral choice:** make the small chart show the ownership rate (65%), which is the figure already on the homepage. Show rent after housing support alongside rent before it. Note that the price-to-earnings ratio leaves out interest rates.

---

### Considered and left out, or weaker still

- **A flattering frame for the current period, which favours the incumbent (Labour) government.** Two examples: the work small chart says "75.3%, higher than in 14 of the other 17 years shown" rather than "below the 2019 peak of 76.1%". The earnings small chart says "£767, the highest of the 5 years measured the same way". Both follow the site's stated rules for comparison windows and series breaks, so I don't count them as strong.
- **Which topics count as everyday life.** Work, pay, housing, holidays and class sizes are covered. Crime and migration, topics that tend to favour the right, are missing, but so are NHS waiting lists, which tend to favour the left. It's a wash.
- **Manufacturing falling from 24.9% of jobs in June 1978.** That is a "deindustrialisation since Thatcher" picture, but 1978 is where the ONS series starts.

In short: no figure on the site is wrong, and most choices are defensible. The lean shows where figures are placed and framed. The tax-and-income card follows the top-1% figure with a rebuttal and shows the poor paying as large a share as the rich. The debt figures come with reassurance: the flattering G7 comparison, "not owed by people", and "deficits are normal". The international pages make UK taxes look average or low. Caveats appear asymmetrically: a reassuring one where a figure looks bad for the UK, none where a figure flatters it, and the ONS's own caveat on the regressive reading is weakened.
