# Audit 10, impartiality: judgement

Judge: neutral; did not build the site. Inputs: `critic-left.md`, `critic-right.md`, `docs/standins/impartiality-rule.md`, and the built pages in `site/` (site version 10255dc). Every example was checked against the built HTML. I did not read the code, other files under `docs/`, or any other review.

Overall: on several sections the two critics object to the same choice from opposite sides: the household-tax lead, the homepage "Who pays?" card, the net-recipients section, the debt-per-household sentence, the "68 of 78 years" chart title and the OECD lead sentence. In each case the page gives both readings' figures side by side. I found no example that breaks the rule in a way that would leave a newcomer with a picture the official evidence does not support.

## Breaches the critics missed

I checked the start year of every comparison, the scale of every chart and the order of every group on the main pages.

- **B1 (upheld, minor): house-price ratio compared from 1997 across a non-comparable year.** `life/house-prices.html` and `life/housing.html` lead with "7.55 times … compared with 3.55 times in 1997", and the chart summary says the ratio "rose from 3.54 times in 1997". The page's own notes say 1997 and 1998 earnings are "weekly pay scaled up to a year, which ONS says is not directly comparable with later years". The first comparable year is 1999 (3.89), so the change is slightly overstated and the comparison crosses a flagged break. The picture (roughly a doubling) is unchanged, so this is not material.
- **B2 (minor): causes in the site's own words on `borrowing.html`.** The section "What affects what a government pays to borrow?" lists causes, and the page also says yields are "not … a verdict" and that the yield is "the closest" measure, all unattributed. This overlaps L5 and R8. It points both ways and is backed by the DMO quote below it.
- **B3 (minor): single-month headline on `borrowing.html`.** "In September 2026 … 5.24%" leads where the rule asks for the latest measured year. The 2025 average (4.58%, also the highest in the G7) is given next to it, so there is no directional effect.
- **B4 (minor, not directional): `spending-history.html` lead sets 1955-56 against 2021-22 across a source break.** The page marks a change of source to PESA in 2020-21. No change is worked out, but the then-and-now sentence invites a reader to compare across that break.
- **B5 (minor, not directional): panel order on `life/jobs-by-industry.html`.** The six industry panels (Manufacturing, Shops, Health, Professional, Education, Construction) follow neither the SIC order nor size in either year, and the page gives no reason. Industries are not one of the groups the rule names, so this is noted, not counted.

Checks that passed:

- **Line charts that start above zero** say so in the caption: spending as a share of GDP (35), earnings (600), work (65, 50, 60), schools (15, 20) and the small charts on the year-by-year page.
- **Charts that start at zero:** all other axes start at zero.
- **Side-by-side panels** share one scale and say so.
- **Order of groups:**
  - income fifths, ages, nations and English regions keep the official order;
  - housing tenure is in English Housing Survey order;
  - spending areas and taxes in one-year charts are ordered largest first;
  - country bars are ranked, and the page says so.
- **Start years** are the first year of each series or the fixed ten-year spending window, apart from B1.
- **Cash, after-inflation and share-of-GDP views** are labelled throughout.
- **Wording:** I found no judging words and no party or politician named as responsible for a change.
