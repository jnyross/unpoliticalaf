# Critic's case: the site leans left

Pages read: `site/index.html`, `spending.html`, `taxes.html`, `who-pays.html`,
`household-tax.html`, `life/index.html`, `methods.html` (built HTML only).

Ten examples, strongest first. To be fair, some choices pull the other way:
`who-pays.html` opens with the top 1% paying 27.2% of Income Tax, the taxes page
gives debt per person (£41,978), and the spending page says spending is 54%
higher after inflation than in 2003-04. The examples below are the strongest
case that the overall effect still nudges a newcomer leftwards. The last three
are clearly weaker than the first seven.

---

## 1. "The poor pay as much tax as the rich" is the headline on three pages

- **Pages:** `index.html` (card "How much household income goes on tax?"),
  `who-pays.html` (hero tiles), `household-tax.html` (lead text and first chart).
- **Exact words:** Home: "Lowest-income fifth 36.7% … Highest-income fifth
  36.2%", with the lowest fifth listed first, as the largest number. Who pays:
  "£8,625 a year in all taxes … by the households of the lowest-income fifth of
  people (36.7% of their gross income)" beside "£51,903 … (36.2% of their gross
  income)". Tax and income: "The share was similar for the lowest-income fifth
  of people (36.7%) and the highest-income fifth (36.2%), but it was made up of
  different taxes." Chart title: "Taxes took a similar share of income for the
  lowest-income fifth and the highest-income fifth in 2023/24".
- **What a newcomer takes from it:** the UK tax system is flat or regressive, so
  the poorest are taxed as hard as the richest. That is a familiar campaigning
  point on the left (calls for lower VAT or more tax on wealth).
- **Why it is loaded:** the bottom fifth's 36.7% depends on dividing spending
  taxes by *gross income*. Many households in that fifth spend more than their
  income: students, retired people living on savings, and people whose income
  is low for a year or under-reported. The site's own table shows the same
  indirect taxes are 16.5% of spending, less than for the middle fifths. That
  appears only in a lower table and a single clause on the home page.
- **Neutral choice:** lead with a measure that is not distorted by spending
  above income (direct taxes, or tax as a share of spending). Or lead with
  ONS's net effect of taxes *and* benefits, the gap falling from 12.2 times to
  3.3 times, which the page places further down. Failing that, give the
  denominator caveat equal weight in the headline, not a subordinate clause.

## 2. The ONS caveat is quoted, then undercut

- **Page:** `household-tax.html`, box "Why can indirect taxes be a large share
  of income for lower-income households?"
- **Exact words:** after the ONS explanation (spending above income, funded
  from savings or borrowing), the site adds: "This is from the ONS bulletin for
  April 2015 to March 2016. The figures on this page are for 2023/24, and the
  latest ONS bulletin does not repeat this explanation."
- **What a newcomer takes from it:** the reassuring explanation is out of date
  and may no longer apply, so the "poor pay most" reading stands.
- **Neutral choice:** present the caveat as a standing feature of the method,
  which it is, since indirect taxes are still modelled from reported spending.
  Note its date in the source line, without a sentence implying it has lapsed.

## 3. A long-run "turning point" from 1988, Thatcher's era

- **Page:** `household-tax.html`, "How has this changed since 1977?" (More on…)
- **Exact words:** "On the older method, the share for the lowest-income fifth
  was below the share for the highest-income fifth in every year from 1977 to
  1987, and above it in every year from 1988 to 2017/18."
- **What a newcomer takes from it:** the tax system turned against the poor in
  the late 1980s and stayed that way for 30 years. That matches a standard
  left-wing account of the Thatcher years (the switch from direct to indirect
  tax, and the poll tax).
- **Neutral choice:** describe each fifth's own trend, as the small panels do.
  Do not build a sentence around the crossover year, which invites a political
  timeline.

## 4. The headline "tax burden" is put in scare quotes and minimised

- **Page:** `taxes.html`, "Receipts and spending since 1948-49"
- **Exact words:** "Taxes alone were 36.0% of GDP in 2025-26, a share news
  reports often call the 'tax burden'. It compares all tax, paid by people and
  businesses, with the size of the economy, so it is not anyone's tax bill".
- **What a newcomer takes from it:** "tax burden" is a media phrase, and the
  number does not really bear on them. The page never says where 36.0% stands
  historically, though it charts back to 1948. The long chart plots *receipts*
  (40.1%), not taxes alone, so the reader cannot easily see whether today's tax
  take is unusually high. The concern that taxes are at a post-war high, raised
  mostly from the right, is never set out.
- **Neutral choice:** "Taxes were 36.0% of GDP in 2025-26, the [highest / Nth
  highest] since [year]", with taxes alone charted over the long run. Use the
  OBR's own term without distancing quotation marks.

## 5. Government debt is described as nobody's problem

- **Page:** `taxes.html`, "Borrowing is not the same as debt"
- **Exact words:** "about £41,978 per person (£100,569 per household). That is
  the debt divided by everyone, an average, not anyone's bill: it is owed by the
  public sector, not by people or households."
- **What a newcomer takes from it:** debt is an abstraction that falls on no one
  in particular, which is the reassuring framing used by those relaxed about
  borrowing. Future taxpayers do service and repay public debt, and the site
  says nothing about that.
- **Neutral choice:** give the per-person figure with a plain "an average across
  everyone", or drop the per-person figure altogether. Avoid the added
  reassurance that households do not owe it.

## 6. Deficits are presented as the normal state of affairs

- **Pages:** `taxes.html`, `index.html`
- **Exact words:** chart headline "Spending was higher than receipts in 68 of
  the 78 years from 1948-49 to 2025-26" (and "65 of the 71 years" on the
  after-inflation view). Also: "Spending was higher than receipts, so the
  government borrowed £134bn to cover the gap." Home: "Borrowing covered
  another £134bn."
- **What a newcomer takes from it:** running a deficit is routine and nothing to
  worry about. The chosen headline is the frequency of deficits. It is not the
  size of today's deficit against the past, nor the forecast, which the chart
  shows still running deficits to 2031-32. The wording "borrowed … to cover the
  gap", like "Borrowing covered", makes borrowing sound like a passive top-up.
- **Neutral choice:** a headline describing the lines' actual paths, as the
  site does elsewhere ("rose until … fell until …"). Or state this year's
  borrowing as a share of GDP against the long-run average.

## 7. Welfare spending is described as "mainly" pensions

- **Pages:** `spending.html` lead text, and every bar label on `index.html` and
  `spending.html`
- **Exact words:** "the largest share, £33, went on social protection: mainly
  State Pension, other benefits and social care." Bar label: "Social protection
  (pensions, benefits, social care)". The page's own figure is "Pensions in old
  age cost £155bn in 2025-26", which "covers more than the State Pension alone".
- **What a newcomer takes from it:** the biggest spending area is mostly
  pensions, so working-age benefits are a small item. But £155bn is about 38% of
  the £407bn, so the State Pension alone is not a majority of social protection,
  and "mainly" overstates it. Working-age benefits are never given their own
  figure, which blunts the right-leaning question of how much goes on them.
- **Neutral choice:** "including the State Pension, other benefits and social
  care", plus a separate line for working-age benefits beside the pensions line.

## 8. The cost of debt interest is played down three times (weaker)

- **Pages:** `index.html`, `spending.html` (three times: the caption, the
  paragraph under the chart, and the Limits section)
- **Exact words:** "this counts interest paid out, and the 'net' figure the OBR
  and IFS often quote, after interest received, is lower". Then the ONS box
  "Why did interest on government debt rise in 2022-23?", whose answer is RPI
  on index-linked gilts.
- **What a newcomer takes from it:** the £11-in-£100 debt-interest figure
  overstates the true cost, and its rise was down to an inflation quirk rather
  than the size of the debt. Both soften the argument for restraint.
- **Neutral choice:** state the gross/net difference once, with the net figure
  given as a number, and add no causal box. If a box is used, include the
  ONS/OBR point about the size of the debt stock as well.

## 9. "Generation rent" is chosen as the housing comparison (weaker)

- **Page:** `life/index.html`, Housing
- **Exact words:** "In 2024-25, 65% of households in England owned their home and
  19% rented privately. The average private rent was £250 a week, social rent
  £129 and the average mortgage payment £242 … In 2008-09, 14% rented
  privately."
- **What a newcomer takes from it:** private renting has grown and renters pay
  more than owners. That is the core of the left's housing argument (rent
  controls, more social housing). The one back-comparison chosen is the rise
  in private renting, not home ownership in 2008-09 or 2003.
- **Neutral choice:** compare the lead figure, ownership, with the same earlier
  year. The methods page promises "the same lead figure on every comparison
  page".

## 10. Pay is framed as stagnant (weakest)

- **Pages:** `index.html` and `life/index.html`, Earnings
- **Exact words:** "April 2021 £761 → April 2025 £767 … about the same", while
  the chart caption notes 2025 is "the highest of the 5 years measured the same
  way".
- **What a newcomer takes from it:** living standards have gone nowhere, a
  stagnation message that suits the left more than the right. The window is
  forced by a coding break, but the headline wording chooses "about the same"
  over "the highest since the series was redefined".
- **Neutral choice:** give both facts in the headline (+0.8%, the highest of the
  five comparable years), or show the longest within-definition stretch.

---

**Count:** ten examples. Numbers 1 to 7 are, in my view, real and material
nudges. Numbers 8 to 10 are marginal, and a fair reader could see them as
neutral or as cutting both ways. I did not find further examples strong enough
to list.
