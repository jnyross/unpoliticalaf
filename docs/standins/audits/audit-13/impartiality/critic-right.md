# Critic: the case that the site leans right

Scope: the built pages `site/index.html`, `spending.html`, `taxes.html`,
`who-pays.html`, `household-tax.html`, `life/index.html` and `methods.html`,
read as a newcomer would see them. I did not read the code or any other review.

This is the strongest honest case I can make. The site has a lot of caveats,
and I say where they soften a point. In most cases the caveat is in smaller
type or in a collapsed "More on…" panel, while the framing I object to is in
the headline, a hero card or a chart title. Ten examples follow, strongest first.

---

## 1. "Who pays?" leads with the top 1%'s share of Income Tax

**Pages:** `index.html` ("Who pays?" card); `who-pays.html` (first hero card and opening paragraph).

**Exact words:** "27.2% of all Income Tax for the year was owed by the top 1% of Income Tax payers in 2023 to 2024." On who-pays: "Higher-income people pay most Income Tax." Section 1's bar chart then shows "Top 10%: share of Income Tax 59.1%", "Top 50%: … 90.1%", "Bottom 50%: … 9.9%".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** the first answer to "who pays?" is "the rich, and far more than their share". This is the line the Taxpayers' Alliance and the right-leaning press use most often against higher taxes on top earners. It covers one tax, Income Tax, which raises about 30% of the total. It leaves out VAT, duties and Council Tax, the taxes that weigh most on poorer households. The figure that answers the page's question (all taxes as a share of income by fifth: 36.7% for the poorest fifth against 36.2% for the richest) is pushed down to section 2 and to another page.

**Neutral choice:** lead "Who pays?" with all taxes by household income fifth, as a share of income, which covers every tax and every household. Or give the HMRC figure equal weight beside a measure of where income and wealth sit, not only beside "share of taxpayers' income", which already leaves out people with too little income to pay Income Tax.

## 2. "Who receives more in benefits than they pay in taxes?" (the net-recipient framing)

**Page:** `who-pays.html`, section 4.

**Exact words:** "In 2023/24, 53.3% of people lived in households that received more in benefits and services than they paid in taxes." The chart title is "The share for people in retired households was higher than for people in non-retired households in every year…".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** most people take out more than they put in, so the system is carried by a shrinking minority. This is the "makers and takers" or "net contributors" argument of right-leaning welfare debate. The number is inflated by counting the NHS and state schooling as "benefits", so any family with a child at school or anyone treated in hospital counts as a taker. The Limits box ("one year at a time … the same person can be on either side at different times") is collapsed by default.

**Neutral choice:** leave this framing out of a section called "Who pays?". If it stays, lead with the lifetime caveat, or show it next to the ONS figure the site itself quotes on household-tax (income gap of 12.2 times before taxes and benefits, 3.3 times after), so that it reads as redistribution working and not as dependency.

## 3. Debt, borrowing and spending turned into a "per household" bill

**Pages:** `taxes.html` ("Borrowing is not the same as debt" and "For scale"); `spending.html` ("For scale").

**Exact words:** "£2,917bn … or about £41,978 per person ( £100,569 per household)". "Borrowing was £1,933 per person, or £4,630 per household." On spending: "about £46,895 per household, or £902 a week."

**What a newcomer takes from it:** each household is on the hook for a six-figure national debt and a spending bill of nearly £1,000 a week. This is the household-budget analogy ("maxing out the nation's credit card") used to argue for austerity. The caveat ("not anyone's bill: it is owed by the public sector") comes after the frightening number, and the arithmetic itself invites the comparison it then disowns. On the same page the spending-as-share-of-GDP chart has a y-axis that "starts at 35, not at zero", which makes the rise from 38.7% to 44.3% look steep. The text says "total spending in 2025-26 was 54% higher than in 2003-04" and gives no matching growth figure for the economy.

**Neutral choice:** give debt as % of GDP only (93.3%, already on the page) and next to the G7, not per household. If per-head figures are kept, put the matching per-household figure for public assets or GDP beside them. Put the "54% higher" next to how much real GDP grew over the same years.

## 4. The ten-year spending window starts after the austerity cuts

**Page:** `spending.html`, "Change by area over ten years: 2015-16 to 2025-26".

**Exact words:** "After adjusting for inflation. The two years are a fixed ten years apart, not chosen for the result." Every area but one shows a rise ("Education 7% rise", "Housing and community amenities 64% rise", "Public order and safety … 33% rise"). Only "Recreation, culture and religion -4% fall".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** spending on almost everything has risen in real terms, so claims of cuts look false. A 2015-16 start skips the 2010-11 to 2015-16 years of real-terms cuts, so the picture flatters the Conservative-led record and goes against the "austerity" story. "Fixed ten years" is still a choice. The site's own every-year table (total spending £1,116.4bn in 2010-11, down to £1,083.1bn in 2013-14) shows the dip the bar chart leaves out.

**Neutral choice:** use the full series the site already holds (2003-04 to 2025-26), or show two windows (before and after 2010) so that the cuts and the later rises are both visible.

## 5. Chart titles that cast deficits as spending exceeding receipts

**Page:** `taxes.html`, "Receipts and spending since 1948-49".

**Exact words:** "Spending was higher than receipts in 68 of the 78 years from 1948-49 to 2025-26" and "Spending was higher than receipts in 65 of the 71 years from 1955-56 to 2025-26". On the same page: "Spending was higher than receipts, so the government borrowed £134bn to cover the gap."

**What a newcomer takes from it:** spending is the active party and keeps outrunning what we can afford, so borrowing is caused by overspending. The same facts could be put as "receipts fell short of spending", which points towards raising revenue. Taking spending as the subject every time leans towards fiscal-restraint conclusions.

**Neutral choice:** "The government borrowed (receipts were below spending) in 68 of 78 years", or name borrowing as the subject: "Borrowing in 68 of 78 years".

## 6. Hero cards that compare cash tax bills, not shares of income

**Page:** `who-pays.html`, hero cards and section 2 bar chart.

**Exact words:** "£51,903 a year in all taxes, on average, by the households of the highest-income fifth" against "£8,625 a year … by the households of the lowest-income fifth". The bar chart is in pounds: "Highest-income fifth £51,903 … Lowest-income fifth £8,625".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** the richest fifth pays six times as much, so the burden falls on them. The shares (36.2% against 36.7%) do appear in small brackets, but the large numbers and the bar lengths show the pound gap. A gap in pounds between groups whose incomes differ six-fold is expected and says little about fairness. Putting it first supports the "the rich already pay enough" reading.

**Neutral choice:** make the share of income the large number in the hero cards and the bar chart, with pounds in the table. The household-tax page already does this.

## 7. Softening how hard indirect tax falls on the poorest

**Pages:** `index.html` ("How much household income goes on tax?"); `household-tax.html`.

**Exact words:** home page: "For the lowest-income fifth, most of this is indirect tax such as VAT ( 21.0% of income, or 16.5% of their spending: the share depends on what it is divided by)". household-tax page: an ONS quote from 2017, which the site admits "the latest ONS bulletin does not repeat", saying low-income households "may maintain their standard of living by funding their expenditure from savings or borrowing, thereby adjusting their lifetime consumption."

**What a newcomer takes from it:** VAT is not really regressive, because measured against spending it is a flat ~16-17% for everyone, and poorer households' high shares are partly a quirk of people spending savings. The site offers a second, lower denominator only for this left-leaning finding. It does not do the same for the top 1% figure in example 1. It also brings back a nine-year-old explanation that the publisher has dropped, and uses it to qualify the result.

**Neutral choice:** report 21.0% of gross income, the same basis as every other figure on the page, on the home page. Keep the "what you divide by" table on the household-tax page. Leave out an old quote the ONS no longer gives, or put an equally prominent official statement of the regressive pattern beside it.

## 8. Debt interest shown gross, at the larger figure

**Pages:** `index.html` and `spending.html`.

**Exact words:** "General public services ( £13 ) includes debt interest ( £11 of the £13 ), counted as interest paid out; the “net” figure the OBR and IFS often quote, after interest received, is lower." On spending, a whole expandable panel asks "Why did interest on government debt rise in 2022-23?"

**What a newcomer takes from it:** £11 of every £100 goes on debt interest, more than defence (£5) and close to education (£10). The figure that is "often quoted" by the OBR and IFS is smaller. The site picks the larger one for the headline and tells the reader the smaller one exists without giving it. It also gives debt interest its own explainer, which no spending area gets except the Covid jump. Both choices push the "debt is crowding out services" argument.

**Neutral choice:** give both figures in the same sentence (gross £X, net £Y), or lead with the OBR's net figure, which the site uses as its source elsewhere on the taxes page.

## 9. "Everyday life" defaults to a flattering window and rosy indicators

**Pages:** `life/index.html` and the home page "Everyday life" block.

**Exact words:** "Start with 2011 compared with 2019, the earliest and latest years in which all five topics can be compared". The employment rate is "higher than in 51 of the other 54 years shown". Visits abroad are "higher than in 40 of the other 43 years shown". Pay is "about the same ( +0.8% )", "compared from 2021", and the dark dot is "the highest of the 5 years measured the same way".

**What a newcomer takes from it:** life has broadly improved: record employment, more holidays abroad, pay holding up. The suggested comparison is exactly the Coalition and Conservative years. Measuring pay from 2021, "the highest of the 5 years", hides that real median pay in 2025 (£767) is still below 2008 (£771, the top of the site's own scale). That is the fifteen-year stagnation central to the left's case. The five topics chosen (jobs, pay, home ownership, holidays, class sizes) leave out poverty, homelessness, NHS waiting times and child poverty, where the trend is worse.

**Neutral choice:** no default pair, or one before and one after a change of government on each side. Say plainly, next to the pay figure, that pay after inflation is still below its 2008 level. Add at least one measure of hardship alongside the measures of comfort.

## 10. Bringing in "tax burden" and stressing that taxes are rising

**Page:** `taxes.html`.

**Exact words:** "Taxes alone were 36.0% of GDP in 2025-26, a share news reports often call the “tax burden”." The forecast lines run to receipts of "42.9%" of GDP in 2031-32, Income Tax rising to "12.2%" of GDP and "£464bn". Two of the official quotes chosen for the tax pages explain rising tax through frozen thresholds: HMRC's "frozen Income Tax thresholds … extension of the freeze at Autumn Budget 2025" (taxes page) and ONS's "fiscal drag … taxpayers can be pushed into higher tax brackets" (household-tax page).

**What a newcomer takes from it:** taxes are a "burden", near record highs and set to climb, partly by stealth. "Burden" is a loaded word, and the site gives it as the term "news reports often call" it instead of using a plain alternative. The explainer panels on tax are about tax going up. There is no matching panel on what the extra revenue paid for.

**Neutral choice:** say "taxes as a share of GDP" and leave out "tax burden", or note that the term is contested. Balance the threshold-freeze quotes with an official explanation of what drove spending, or present both in the same neutral form.

---

**Note on strength:** examples 1 to 4 are, I think, strong: each is a prominent framing choice with a clear right-leaning reading and a ready neutral alternative. Examples 5 to 8 are real but smaller, and the site's caveats partly answer them. Examples 9 and 10 are the weakest. Example 9 could equally be read as favouring whichever party is in office, and in example 10 the term and the forecasts come from the OBR and the press, not the site's own invention.
