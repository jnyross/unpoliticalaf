What this site is for

This site shows where UK public money goes, in plain words. It uses official figures and nothing else. It does not say if any spend is right or wrong. It does not back any party.

How to check a figure

  • Find the official source publication, table and version in the source note.
  • Check the fiscal year, unit, scale and definitions for that cell.
  • Read the source notes: an actual, estimate and forecast are not the same. A forecast is not observed spending.
  • Check reconciliation with the published total. Allow for rounding, exclusions and scope differences. Rounded parts may not add up exactly.

When figures are published, they can later be revised. The dates below show which files we use. No next release date has been recorded in this port.

Where the numbers come from

Most numbers come from one report by the Treasury: Public Expenditure Statistical Analyses 2026 (PESA). These are Accredited Official Statistics. They cover all public bodies across the whole UK. That means central government, local councils and firms the state owns.

The figures every ten years since 1945 come from the Office for Budget Responsibility (OBR). The OBR is the official body that checks the government's figures. Its Public finances databank, October 2026 gives spending, income, borrowing and debt. Its Historical public finances database gives spending on five areas. The OBR says the second one is not National Statistics. It joins up old sources, and it made its own choices to do that.

The Treasury counts years through 2025-26 as outturn. Later total-spending years are forecasts. The price base and forecast date come from the notes in PESA 4_1!B3, 4_1!B51 and 4_1!B52. The newer OBR sheet has mixed dates in its notes. It names both March and October forecasts. We keep the conflict, rather than call all values observed data. Each series cites its own numerator and GDP update notes. See Public finances since 1900, B2 and B139 to B148.

The older OBR area series joins sources. Its status is uncertain. Its 2022-23 extrapolations are estimates; our decade tables end in 2015-16 (Contents, D51 to D56).

Each file has a unique code, called a hash. The full codes are in the data file. You can download the files and check the codes match. If they match, the files have not been changed.

The record behind each value

The data file has a provenance record for each value. Its source_id links to the source file, full hash, publication and dates in sources. The record names the exact cell, year, unit, scale, price basis, UK coverage and accounting scope. It also names the label, year and unit cells used to check its meaning.

The source_cells records keep raw values, formulas and formats. A blank, a literal dash and a numeric zero stay distinct. The source notes do not define every dash in the PESA tables. We keep those as missing. A number format that shows zero as a dash does not turn that zero into a missing value.

The formulae and derived records give each formula and its ordered operands. Money in Table 5.2 is in millions; we divide by a thousand to show billions. Changes that cross a known definition break are not given as percentages. The data record names the source note for each break. Shares use spending on services as the whole. Changes use the same series in two years. Neither calculation fills a missing value.

The OBR publication dates in the catalogue were read from file headers. They have not been verified as release dates. Their retrieval dates are recorded too.

How the numbers are checked

A script reads the Treasury files. No number is typed in by hand. The build checks the following sums before it writes the pages:

  • Each value is checked against its source label, year, unit and accounting scope.
  • PESA sums use stored source rounding steps. The data file shows each residual and its budget.
  • OBR identities use stored ratios, not the one-decimal values shown on the page.
  • A sum with missing operands is not tested. The data file names each missing cell.
  • OBR and PESA use different vintages. Their difference has no established error bound.

Then a second script reads every number on the page. It checks each one against the cell it names in the source file. The source files and sums can be checked again with the build commands in the README.

Residuals and limits

A residual is the signed sum of the source operands. We do not change a value to make a sum fit. For PESA, the budget is half the stored rounding step for each operand, added together. The checker first checks that each value lies on that source grid. This is a rounding budget, not a bound on how well the source measures spending.

OBR ratios keep more digits than we show. Their identities use those stored digits and a small allowance for computer arithmetic. The one-decimal format is not a source error bound. Checks with missing operands stay untested. The source's “other” series is already a residual, so its sum is not an independent measure of accuracy.

The OBR and PESA comparison has different vintages and GDP bases. We show its difference with no pass/fail bound. There is no established half-point error budget.

The full reconciliations ledger in the data file has all years, signed operands, budgets, residuals and exclusions. Selected items sit inside function totals. They are never added to those totals. The historical pensioner subsets are excluded from the function sum to avoid counting them twice.

Taxes, receipts and debt

The Taxes page uses the OBR's current receipts and aggregate tables. The source version is Public finances databank, October 2026. Download the selected tax data for full hashes, exact cells, years, units, status and formulas. The official OBR workbook download is the source. The source notes keep each table's own status and GDP notes.

The six receipt groups use ordered source rows: income tax Q+R+S; National Insurance AB; VAT C+D; corporation tax U+V; other taxes AH minus those named groups; and receipts outside taxes AG−AH. Their shares use all current public receipts AG as the whole, not tax AH. No total is added to its own parts. Negative source values keep their signs.

VAT includes refunds and is a source-based grouping, not household VAT burden. The notes do not explicitly settle refund classification within National Accounts taxes. Corporation tax includes related charges. Other taxes is a remainder, partly from a mixed line that is not itemised. Non-tax is a difference, not the mixed AF line relabelled. A sum that closes does not prove these classifications.

The balance uses spending D minus receipts C and checks borrowing O in the same OBR aggregate table. PESA is a different vintage and presentation. We do not subtract OBR receipts from PESA spending. Debt X is a stock at end March; borrowing is an annual accrual flow. Debt changes need not equal borrowing. Published debt shares use March-centred GDP. We keep them as published, even when a cash ratio differs.

The history shows total receipts, spending, borrowing and debt, not history by tax type. It starts in 1999-00, the first year of the current receipt table. It does not splice in the older historical receipt data. Outturn can be revised. Later forecasts are shown apart from outturn, with their own source notes. No inflation-adjusted tax view has been verified. A GDP share is not a real-terms measure, and can rise when GDP falls.

These tables show receipts by tax type. They do not show how payments are split between people, or who ultimately bears the cost.

Words used on this site

Total spending
The Treasury calls this Total Managed Expenditure. It is all the public sector spends, including building work and interest.
Spending on services
Total spending split into areas such as health and defence. It leaves out some book-keeping items that do not belong to one area.
Adjusted for inflation
Prices rise over time, so £1 bought more in the past. We show older amounts in 2025-26 prices so you can compare years fairly. This uses the same price measure as the Treasury tables.
Share of GDP
GDP is the value of all the goods and services made in the UK in a year. This shows spending as a part of that.
Comparing years
We compare in steps of ten years, counting back from 2025-26. So we use 2015-16, 2005-06 and so on, back to 1945-46. We do not pick years by hand.
Areas
The areas are the ones the United Nations uses, called COFOG. We give each one a plain name next to its official name.
