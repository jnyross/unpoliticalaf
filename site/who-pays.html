Who pays tax?

Two official views answer different questions. Do not join them into one tax bill.

Who owed Income Tax in 2023/24?

These shares are for tax year 2023 to 2024, estimated outturn. They show Income Tax due, not cash received. HMRC published these estimates on 15 July 2026. They cover UK people with a positive Income Tax bill. People with no Income Tax bill are excluded.

HMRC ranks people by income assessable for tax. This is before deductions and allowances. It is not a rank of wealth. It does not count all income.

The top 1% is part of the top 10%. The top 10% is part of the top 50%. The bottom 50% and top 10% do not overlap. These groups refer to taxpayers with a positive Income Tax bill, not all adults. Do not add their shares. Tax due is not cash collected. This is Income Tax only, not all UK taxes.

How do taxes compare with household income?

ONS uses 2023/24: the financial year ending 2024. It published these figures on 25 September 2025. These are completed-year survey/model estimates, not a forecast. They cover UK people in private households.

ONS ranks people by equivalised household disposable income. This starts with household income after direct taxes. ONS adjusts it for household size and needs. It then forms five groups. Each has about the same number of people, not households. These are not the HMRC taxpayer groups.

Gross income includes cash benefits. Disposable income is gross income after direct taxes. Each row shows one group's taxes. The shares use that group's gross household income as the whole.

Why the bottom group needs care

Deferred lump-sum tax payments can distort the groups. They can leave people with high gross income but low disposable income. These people can enter the bottom group. These payments need not match a typical year's tax bill.

People can spend more than their current income. They can use savings or borrowing. Temporary low income, sampling and measurement issues also matter. A high indirect-tax share of income is not a tax rate on purchases or a lifetime tax burden. Compare it with the spending view below.

Who sends the tax, and who bears its cost?

How is the tax collected?

Employers normally deduct Income Tax from employees' pay. They also deduct National Insurance. They send this money to HMRC. They can also owe employer National Insurance. PAYE: deductions and paying HMRC

VAT-registered businesses normally pay HMRC the difference between VAT charged to customers and VAT paid to suppliers. They do not send the whole purchase price as VAT. VAT: business responsibilities

Who sends the tax is not the same question as who bears its cost. These rules explain how tax is sent. They do not tell us who bears the cost.

What these sources can tell us

These sources explain how tax is sent. They do not model tax costs. Read the household analysis for the model's assumptions and limits. It has its own sources.

Current official guidance retrieved 30 October 2026. No publication date is established. No rates are used here. This guidance is not the 2023/24 distribution data. The next guidance update is unknown.

ONS assigns final and intermediate indirect taxes fully to consumers in this model. This includes employer National Insurance. Employee and self-employed National Insurance are direct taxes.

The actual cost can fall on producers, workers, buyers and others. Their shares can vary. ONS assumes that consumers bear the full cost of indirect taxes. This is an assumption. It is not a settled fact. The National Insurance receipts total joins several parts. It must not be mapped wholly to either ONS group.

The linked technical report covers FYE 2019. It was published on 25 June 2020. Sections 7 and 10 explain these assumptions and limits. The QMI is dated 15 June 2018. These are the latest linked reports we found in the sources we kept. They are older reports. Some details may have changed. Current workbook notes take priority.

HMRC and ONS cover different people. They measure different taxes. Do not combine them. They cannot give a top-1% all-tax burden. Do not take an average across the panels. Do not match them to tax receipts.
